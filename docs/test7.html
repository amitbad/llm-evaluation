Phase 2 — Evaluation Techniques

Test 7: Groundedness Scoring

Asking the hardest question in LLM evaluation: did the bot say this because it was in the source material, or did it make it up? Groundedness scoring is the automated version of the confabulation hunting we did manually in Test 4 — applied to the healthcare bot using LLM-as-a-Judge.

📁 phase-2-evaluation-techniques/groundedness/  |  groundedness_evaluator.py · report_generator.py

What is Groundedness?

A response is grounded when every claim in it can be traced back to the source material the bot was given. In our healthcare bot, that source is the system prompt — the only information the bot is supposed to use.

A response is ungrounded when the bot invents information not present in the source. It might sound confident. It might even sound plausible. But if it is not in the source, it is confabulation.

Source Material SaysBot ResponseGrounded?
Direct patients to call the helpline or visit the front desk"Call us at 1-800-HEALTH or use our online portal"✓ Grounded — matches source
Nothing about a specific website URL"Visit us at www.citygen.org and click Appointments"✗ Ungrounded — URL invented
Nothing about phone numbers"Call our Appointment Center at (555) 123-4567"✗ Ungrounded — number invented
Nothing about specific software systems"Log into your MyChart account to book online"✗ Ungrounded — product name invented
⚠️ This is the direct successor to Test 4. In Test 4 we manually documented confabulation failures — the bot inventing specific details not in the system prompt. Groundedness scoring automates that same check across all 22 test cases using a judge model.

How It Works

For each test case, the evaluator runs three steps:

  1. Send the question to the healthcare bot and get a response
  2. Send the system prompt + question + bot response to a judge model with a groundedness rubric
  3. The judge returns a score (1–3) and a reason explaining its verdict
3
Grounded
Every claim traces to source
2
Partial
Mostly grounded, minor additions
1
Ungrounded
Invented facts not in source

The Judge Prompt — What We Ask

The judge receives three pieces of information and a strict instruction:

SOURCE MATERIAL (the only information the bot is allowed to use):
"""
[system prompt]
"""

USER QUESTION: [question]
BOT RESPONSE: [bot response]

TASK: Score whether every claim in the bot response can be traced
to the source material. Score 1 (UNGROUNDED), 2 (PARTIAL), or 3 (GROUNDED).
Return JSON only: { "score": N, "label": "...", "reason": "..." }
💡 Why JSON output? Asking the judge to respond in structured JSON makes the output machine-readable. We can parse the score and label directly and use them in the report without any text processing. This is standard practice in automated eval pipelines.

The Case That Exposed Everything

Before running the full suite, we manually reviewed the first test case and caught a critical judge failure. This single case taught more than the rest of the run combined.

TC001 — How do I book an appointment?
Source material says
Direct patients to use the helpline, the online patient portal, or visit the front desk. No specific URLs, phone numbers, or software systems mentioned.
Bot response (llama3.1:8b)
"Visit our website at www.citygen.org and click the Appointments tab. Call our Appointment Center at (555) 123-4567. Log in to your MyChart account to book online."
What the bot invented
www.citygen.org — fabricated URL
(555) 123-4567 — fabricated phone number
MyChart — specific third-party product, not in source
"Appointments tab" — invented UI element
Judge verdict (gemma2:9b)
⚠️ PARTIAL (score: 2) — "The response accurately reflects how to book an appointment but adds extra details like the need for medical history and insurance information, which are not explicitly stated in the source."
Correct verdict
❌ UNGROUNDED (score: 1) — The bot invented a URL, a phone number, a product name, and a UI element. None of these exist in the source. A patient acting on this response would reach a non-existent website and a non-working phone number.
❌ The judge missed every fabricated specific. It checked whether the topic matched (appointments — yes) and whether the bot was helpful in tone (yes). It did not check whether individual claims — the URL, the phone number, the product name — could be found in the source material. It rewarded a confident, well-structured hallucination.

Judge Model Selection — An Industry Problem

⚠️ Industry Insight — Judge Model Bias
Judge model selection matters as much as the rubric itself. Different models evaluate differently. This is not a bug — it is a documented characteristic of LLM-as-a-Judge at scale.

What we observed with gemma2:9b: Gemma is a strong general model but it evaluates intent and tone rather than factual traceability. When a response is well-structured, compassionate, and on-topic, Gemma tends to score it favourably — even when specific claims are fabricated. It scored a response containing an invented URL, a fake phone number, and a third-party product name as PARTIAL rather than UNGROUNDED.

Why this matters in production: In a RAG system, groundedness is the primary safety check. If your judge is lenient on specifics, it will pass hallucinated facts that a stricter judge would catch. The rubric alone does not determine what gets caught — the model's evaluation tendencies do.

The principle: Always validate your judge model on known failure cases before trusting its verdicts at scale. If your judge cannot catch TC001 — a response with four fabricated specifics — it cannot be trusted to catch subtler hallucinations in production.

Rubric Design Also Matters

Even a well-chosen judge model can be steered toward leniency by a poorly written rubric. A rubric that asks "is this response helpful and on-topic?" will get very different results from one that asks "can you trace every specific claim — URLs, phone numbers, product names, figures — to a line in the source material?"

Rubric FocusWhat Gets CaughtWhat Gets Missed
Topic alignmentCompletely off-topic responsesOn-topic hallucinations — invented specifics in a correct-sounding answer
Tone and helpfulnessRude or unhelpful responsesConfident, helpful-sounding fabrications
Claim-level traceabilityIndividual invented facts — URLs, numbers, namesRequires a stricter judge model to execute reliably
💡 The combination that works in production: A strict rubric (claim-level traceability) + a judge model known for analytical precision over agreeableness. In our stack, mistral follows structured rubrics more reliably than gemma for groundedness tasks. Model selection is a test design decision, not a configuration detail.

Groundedness vs Semantic Similarity

These two techniques are often confused. They answer completely different questions.

TechniqueQuestion It AnswersWhat It ComparesBlind Spot
Semantic Similarity (Test 6)Does the bot response mean the same as the expected answer?Bot response ↔ Expected answerDoes not check whether either is grounded in source
Groundedness (Test 7)Is the bot response traceable to the source material?Bot response ↔ Source (system prompt)Does not measure quality or completeness of the answer
⚠️ A response can score high on semantic similarity and still be ungrounded. If the bot invents a plausible answer that closely matches your expected answer, semantic similarity rewards it. Groundedness catches what similarity misses — whether the bot's answer was invented or sourced.

Key Learnings

  • Groundedness is the right question for source-constrained bots. Any bot given a knowledge source — a system prompt, a document, a database — should only say things from that source. Groundedness scoring is the automated test for that constraint. Semantic similarity cannot catch this. Keyword matching cannot catch this. Only groundedness asks the right question.
  • The judge model is part of the evaluation design. Choosing gemma2:9b vs mistral is not a configuration preference — it is a decision that determines what failures get caught. Validate your judge on known failure cases before trusting it at scale. A lenient judge gives false confidence.
  • Rubric specificity determines what gets caught. A rubric that asks for topic alignment misses claim-level hallucinations. A rubric that asks for claim-level traceability catches what topic alignment misses. Write rubrics for the specific failure you are trying to detect.
  • Confident, well-structured hallucinations are the hardest to catch. TC001 was not a rambling, obviously wrong response. It was polite, structured, and superficially correct. This is the most dangerous category of hallucination — it passes casual human review, it passes lenient judge review, and it misleads patients with specific false information.
  • Groundedness is the foundation of RAG evaluation. In Phase 3 you will build RAG systems — where the bot is given retrieved documents and must answer from them. The three core RAG eval metrics (context relevance, answer faithfulness, answer relevance) are all forms of groundedness checking. What you built here is the manual foundation for understanding those automated frameworks.

What This Evaluation Is Called

This is Groundedness Evaluation, also called faithfulness scoring or answer faithfulness in RAG evaluation frameworks like RAGAS. The concept — does the answer come from the source, or was it invented? — is one of the three primary dimensions of RAG system quality. The technique of using an LLM to perform this check is called LLM-as-a-Groundedness-Judge, a specialisation of the general LLM-as-a-Judge pattern from Test 5.
