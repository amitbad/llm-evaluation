## Beginner Explanation

### 1. Ground truth starts with the question and the evidence

Suppose the question is about July 15, 2026. The approved policy says the minimum for Standard Savings was ₹5,000 that day. That’s what you can verify before you look at any reply. We call the best-supported fact or expected behavior for a case its **ground truth**.

Now suppose the customer asks about the ATM limit and the supplied policy says nothing about it. You can still expect the assistant to say it can’t confirm the limit. The missing evidence tells you how to handle the question. It doesn’t establish that the bank has no limit.

Keep three things separate as you work. The **fact** is the requirement you can verify. For July 15, it’s the ₹5,000 minimum. A **reference answer** is one acceptable way to say it. Try “The Standard Savings minimum is ₹5,000.”

An **evaluation label** is your judgment of a reply you actually read. “Keep five thousand rupees in the account” can earn PASS if it’s clear which account you’re discussing. A reply that asserts ₹10,000 earns FAIL. The policy fact stays the same when the label changes.

What if one reviewer likes “maintain” and another likes “keep”? Ask them to point to the same policy line and say what the reply needs to mean. A word preference doesn’t decide a bank requirement.

Teams sometimes call reviewed evidence **gold evidence** or a reviewed answer a **gold reference**. This means someone checked it for that case. If you later find an error, you can correct it and record why.

Some questions have an answer you can’t verify yet. A policy can change with time, omit an ATM limit, or disagree with another approved document. If an excerpt simply omits the limit, you can test whether the assistant admits the gap.

If two equally authoritative policies disagree and neither takes priority, you can’t verify either policy claim. Mark the case NEEDS REVIEW and ask the person responsible for the policy to resolve it.

### 2. Read the Harbor Leaf policy records

Think of the bank publishing a new fee sheet. A draft in someone’s folder might look just as official as the version the Savings Policy team signed off. An **approved source** is one the bank authorizes for the subject at hand.

Leave a trail another reviewer can follow. Write down the document ID, version, section, approving owner, and dates. This record of where the evidence came from is called **provenance**. Here the policy register, the bank’s record of approved documents, gives the Savings Policy team authority over Standard Savings balances and fees. It also requires the assistant to keep PINs private. Use the excerpts below as the full evidence for the examples that cite them.

- **SV-1, Savings Policy version 1, §2, approved.** Effective January 1 through June 30, 2026, inclusive. Standard Savings requires a minimum of ₹4,000. A ₹80 monthly fee applies when the balance is below ₹4,000.
- **SV-2, Savings Policy version 2, §2, approved.** Effective July 1, 2026. Standard Savings requires a minimum of ₹5,000. A ₹100 monthly fee applies when the balance is below ₹5,000. At the minimum, the below-minimum fee does not apply. This excerpt is silent about ATM limits and special student waivers.
- **HELP-DRAFT, help-center draft, unapproved.** Edited July 10, 2026. It suggests that Standard Savings requires ₹3,000. Its editing date establishes when someone saved the draft. Approval and effective dates determine which policy governs.
- **FEE-A and FEE-B, fee addenda, both approved.** Both list the same Savings Policy owner and become effective August 1, 2026. A says students are eligible for a below-minimum fee waiver. B says students are ineligible. The register gives no precedence or supersession between them. The baseline minimum and fee in SV-2 still apply to questions that do not depend on these addenda.

Which day is the customer asking about? That’s the **as-of date**. If they ask about “today,” write down the date the case uses.

1. Check that the policy covers the account and subject.
2. Check who approved it and whether that owner can set the rule.
3. Check that the policy was in force on the date asked about.
4. Look for another document with the same authority that gives a different rule. If the register says which one replaces the other, record that. If it doesn’t settle the disagreement, mark NEEDS REVIEW.

A file’s edit or download date shows when someone worked with it. Use the approval and effective dates to decide which policy applies.

Notice how the two approved savings versions can both be accurate for their dates. A reply about June 15 can correctly say ₹4,000 even if the evaluator opens the July policy today. By contrast, the two August addenda overlap in date, scope, and authority. Escalate that conflict because neither of those filters breaks the tie.

### 3. Build a reference answer from the evidence

Imagine handing another reviewer a short example of what you’d accept. Write the fact or action the reply needs to convey, then write one way to say it. Attach the source, section, version, and date to that **reference answer** so the next reviewer can check it.

You may also want the assistant to point the customer to the source. That pointer is a **citation**. A source number next to a wrong claim doesn’t make the claim right.

For “What is the Standard Savings minimum on July 15, 2026?”, all three replies meet the same fact requirement under SV-2 §2.

1. “The Standard Savings minimum is ₹5,000.” This names the right account and requirement directly.
2. “Keep at least ₹5,000 in Standard Savings.” “At least” correctly includes the threshold and amounts above it.
3. “Maintain a minimum of five thousand rupees in Standard Savings.” This spells out the amount and preserves its meaning and scope.

Keep those three reviewed wordings together and you have a **reference answer set**. It gives a reviewer examples of the same meaning. If the assistant phrases it a fourth way, read what it says before deciding.

When would you really need an exact match? One case is a software connection that expects a fixed status value. Another is an **extractive** task, where you ask for text copied from the source. If you ask for the amount exactly as printed in “Standard Savings requires a minimum of ₹5,000,” expect the text “₹5,000.” In a **generative** task, the assistant writes an answer in its own words. “Five thousand rupees” can be fine for a customer explanation. Pick the rule that fits the job.

Now try writing an acceptable reply for the ATM limit. “I can’t confirm the daily ATM withdrawal limit from the supplied policy” tells the customer where the answer stops. Holding back a claim you can’t support is **abstention**. If the task allows it, the assistant can suggest checking an approved ATM policy. A guessed number should fail. Save the exact excerpt so another reviewer can check what it leaves out.

The August waiver case is different. The documents give opposite answers. A cautious reply can acknowledge that conflict, but the policy owner still needs to settle it. If you want to score the assistant’s handling of the conflict, write a separate expected behavior for that.

### 4. Build a benchmark that can teach you something

Suppose you want to repeat the July question tomorrow. Save the question, what the assistant had to go on, and what a good reply should do. That saved scenario is a **test case**. Put several cases together and you have a **dataset**.

To compare answers fairly across runs, agree on the questions you cover, rules for scoring, and which version of the dataset to use. Together these form a **benchmark**. When you apply that benchmark to a set of replies, you’re doing an **evaluation run**. One case can show how a rule works. A benchmark lets you check how that rule and the assistant behave across different situations.

Begin with two cases. B01 checks a straightforward supported answer. B02 checks a correct paraphrase. They cover one question in two forms, which is enough to show why exact matching can fail. Use this compact benchmark sheet as you expand the set. Each row has a stable case ID, question, as-of date, source, saved reply, expected label, and reason. For every row, SV-1 and SV-2 refer to §2 and the policy register given above.

- **B01, supported.** Ask “What is the Standard Savings minimum?” as of July 15, 2026, using SV-2. Reply “The minimum is ₹5,000.” PASS. The minimum matches the current approved policy.
- **B02, accepted paraphrase.** Ask the same question using the same date and source. Reply “Keep five thousand rupees in Standard Savings.” PASS. The amount and account are unchanged.
- **B03, incorrect reply.** Ask the same question using SV-2 on July 15. Reply “The minimum is ₹10,000.” FAIL. The reply conflicts with the approved amount.
- **B04, historical policy.** Ask “What minimum applied on June 15, 2026?” using SV-1. Reply “The minimum was ₹4,000.” PASS. The as-of date is inside version 1’s effective interval.
- **B05, boundary.** Ask “Is the below-minimum fee due at exactly ₹5,000?” on July 15 using SV-2. Reply “No below-minimum fee applies.” PASS. The fee applies below the threshold.
- **B06, tempting wrong fee.** Ask “What fee applies at a balance of ₹4,999?” on July 15 using SV-2. Reply “A ₹100 fee applies every day.” FAIL. The amount happens to match, but the fee is monthly.
- **B07, unsupported question.** Ask “What is the daily ATM withdrawal limit?” on July 15. The evidence packet contains only SV-2. Reply “I can’t confirm that from the supplied policy.” PASS. Abstaining is the approved behavior for this packet.
- **B08, unresolved evidence.** Ask “Does the student waiver apply on August 3, 2026?” using both approved addenda, FEE-A and FEE-B. Reply “The student fee waiver applies.” NEEDS REVIEW. The register leaves the conflict unresolved.

B01, B02, and B04 are **positive cases** for a correct response. B03 and B06 are **negative cases** for an evaluator that detects known wrong replies. B05 is a **boundary case**, an input at the edge of a rule where “below” and “at or below” produce different results. B07 checks an unsupported question. B08 checks that you can keep uncertainty visible. Production questions also vary in form, context, topic, and difficulty. This sheet is a teaching set. It contains eight saved cases that test selected distinctions, so it cannot establish production readiness.

Give each real benchmark row a stable ID and include the question, date or account context, source authority and excerpt, expected behavior, acceptable variants, and a short reason reviewers can apply. Keep the saved model reply and your actual run label with the run. A test can have a known expectation before any assistant reply exists. Separate a wrong policy answer from a response that never arrived, as in M02-T01.

### 5. Check the benchmark itself

Imagine checking whether a new employee can handle the savings help desk by asking only “What is the minimum?” A good answer still leaves you wondering what they’ll do with conflicting documents or an unsupported request. **Benchmark validity** asks whether your cases and rules measure the behavior you set out to check.

Write a short scoring guide, called a **rubric**. In B06, tell the reviewer to check both the fee amount and how often it applies. Otherwise one reviewer may accept any answer with ₹100 while another notices that “every day” changes the claim.

Watch for errors in the reference data. If a July row mistakenly cites SV-1, a correct ₹5,000 reply might fail. If a fee reference says “₹100 when low,” reviewers may disagree about what low means and when it is charged. Fix the source or make the cutoff and frequency explicit. If two reviewers disagree, give them the same evidence and ask for their reasons. Use a policy owner for disagreements about authority. Retest with the revised rubric and record who approved the change.

The automated pitfalls from M02-T01 still apply. A strict phrase comparison can produce a **false failure** on a correct paraphrase. A weak keyword check can produce a **false pass** on “₹100 every day” because it sees the right amount. Read the entire claim against the evidence. For an unfamiliar reply, route it to review rather than assuming that absence from a list is a failure. Review can mean the source is unresolved or the reply is new to a simple checker. Give the two reasons separate labels when you report them.

Imagine giving a student the answer key to the quiz, then using their score to decide how they’ll do on new questions. If reserved questions, answers, or near-copies reach the assistant or its tuning process and make the test unrealistically easy, that’s **data leakage**.

Giving the assistant an approved policy is part of its job here. Giving it the reserved cases and their answer key changes the test. Use some cases to improve the assistant and save other realistic questions for the release check. Look for copied and lightly reworded questions across the two groups. Try fresh wording before you claim an improvement.

Version the benchmark, for example `HLB-SAV-1`, and record the exact policy excerpts and rubric version used. To repair a label, explain the defect, record the old and new labels and reasons, have another reviewer check the change, and publish a new version. Preserve the original results with their benchmark IDs. Comparing a score from eight easy cases with one from a larger, tougher sheet may reflect the change in cases rather than a model change. To compare two assistant versions, rerun both on the same frozen benchmark and report counts, including NEEDS REVIEW. If you publish an aggregate, state which cases are included in its denominator.

## Worked Demonstration

### Example 1. Accept a paraphrase and repair the check

**User question.** “What minimum balance do I need for Standard Savings on July 15, 2026?”

**Approved reference.** SV-2 §2 requires ₹5,000 from July 1, 2026.

**Actual LLM response.** “Keep five thousand rupees in Standard Savings.”

**Assessment.** Ask three small questions. Does the reply name the right account? Yes. Is “five thousand rupees” ₹5,000? Yes. Does “keep” convey the requirement? Here it does. Mark PASS. A literal comparison with “The minimum is ₹5,000” would wrongly reject the reply. Keep the paraphrase as a reviewed example and leave room in the scoring guide for other wording with the same meaning.

### Example 2. Resolve two conflicting amounts by date and authority

**User question.** “What minimum balance applied on June 15, 2026?”

**Approved reference.** SV-1 §2 says ₹4,000 through June 30. SV-2 §2 says ₹5,000 starting July 1. HELP-DRAFT is unapproved.

**Actual LLM response.** “The Standard Savings minimum was ₹4,000.”

**Assessment.** Put your finger on June 15 in the calendar. SV-1 covers that date and gives ₹4,000. SV-2 starts after it, and the draft was never approved. Mark PASS. Move the question to July 15 and try the same ₹4,000 reply again. It now fails. Save the question date and policy version so another reviewer knows why.

### Example 3. Separate a missing answer from a policy conflict

**User question.** “What is the ATM limit?” The second customer asks, “Did the student waiver apply on August 3?”

**Approved reference.** The ATM case supplies only SV-2 §2, which does not cover withdrawals. The waiver case supplies FEE-A and FEE-B, with equally authoritative conflicting claims.

**Actual LLM response.** For the ATM question, “I can’t confirm the limit from the supplied policy.” For the waiver question, “The student fee waiver applies.”

**Assessment.** Ask what you can check in each case. For the ATM question, you can verify that the excerpt omits the limit and the assistant says so. Mark PASS for that behavior. A guessed amount would fail. For the waiver question, you can verify that the documents disagree, but you can’t verify the claim that the waiver applies. Mark NEEDS REVIEW, keep both addenda, and ask the policy owner to settle the conflict. To check whether the assistant explains the disagreement to the customer, write that expectation as a separate test.

### Step 4 — Represent a case as a dictionary

Picture an index card with a labeled box for the ID, another for the question, and one for the source. A **dictionary** has those named fields inside curly braces. A field name is called a **key**. A colon joins it to its value, and commas separate the pairs.

`case["id"]` reads the ID from that card. The “Evidence” line reads two fields, the source and the question date. On the last line, Python reads `reply` and `reference` from the same card and compares their text. Keeping all of this together gives the next reviewer a trail to follow. You may hear someone call this **auditable**.

Copy the block into a fresh editor or replace the previous file, then run it.

```python
case = {
    "id": "B01",
    "question": "What is the Standard Savings minimum?",
    "as_of": "2026-07-15",
    "source": "SV-2",
    "reference": "The minimum is ₹5,000.",
    "reply": "The minimum is ₹5,000.",
}

print("Case:", case["id"])
print("Evidence:", case["source"], case["as_of"])
print("Exact match:", case["reply"] == case["reference"])
```

```text
Case: B01
Evidence: SV-2 2026-07-15
Exact match: True
```

Python used the ID and evidence fields when displaying the result. The last line compares the two strings retrieved from the dictionary.


### Step 6 — Check the evidence status before checking wording

Before comparing the wording, ask whether someone verified the evidence. Read `!=` as “is different from.” If the evidence value differs from `"resolved"`, the first branch prints NEEDS REVIEW. A human reviewer sets that value using the policy checks you already practiced.

Each colon starts an indented block. `if` tries the first condition. If it’s false, `elif` tries the next one, which looks for the reply in the accepted list. If neither condition applies, `else` prints the remaining outcome. Run this once with approved evidence, then change only the evidence decision.

Copy the block into a fresh editor or replace the previous file, then run it.

```python
case = {
    "source": "SV-2",
    "as_of": "2026-07-15",
    "evidence": "resolved",
    "reply": "The minimum is ₹5,000.",
    "accepted": ["The minimum is ₹5,000."],

print("Evidence:", case["source"], case["as_of"])
```

### Step 7 — Produce a readable evaluation report

Use the eight reviewed cases above. This miniature checker looks up known good and known bad saved replies. It does not judge an unfamiliar reply for factual accuracy. The cases show the expected behavior within the supplied evidence packets, including a reviewed abstention for B07.

You need the same order of checks for all eight cases. Put the checks in a **function**, a reusable block of instructions. `def assess(case):` names the block and says it works with one case at a time. The indented instructions run whenever you call it.

Read the checks from top to bottom. If the source is unresolved, end the check there. Otherwise test the list of accepted replies, then the list of rejected replies. If you can’t find the wording in either list, ask for review. Each `return` stops that call and hands back a label and a reason, an ordered pair called a **tuple**. The line `outcome, reason = assess(case)` puts those two results into two names in that order.

The cards keep the dates, sources, and reviewed replies. Empty brackets `[]` mean there are no items in that list yet. The report loop calls `assess` for each card. After the dot in `outcomes.append(outcome)`, `append` means “add this label to the end of the list.” Once the loop finishes, `outcomes.count("PASS")` counts the entries equal to PASS. The last two lines do the same for FAIL and NEEDS REVIEW, so you can see every kind of outcome.

Copy the whole block into a fresh editor or replace the previous file, then run it.

```python
def assess(case):
```

## Knowledge Check

1. For the July 15 case, identify the fact, one reference answer, and the label for a reply giving ₹10,000.
2. Why might “You must maintain at least ₹5,000” pass a case whose reference uses “The minimum is ₹5,000”?
3. A draft created July 10 mentions ₹3,000, while approved SV-2 began July 1. Which source governs a July 15 answer?
4. Why is a fee question at exactly ₹5,000 valuable in a benchmark?
5. What label should the benchmark give a supported abstention for an ATM question and a factual answer under the unresolved August waiver conflict?
6. How can a checker miss the error in “₹100 charged every day” when the policy says ₹100 monthly?
7. How would putting the reserved release questions and answer key into the assistant's tuning data affect the evaluation?
8. Two reported scores used different benchmark versions after a wrong reference was repaired. What comparison can you make next?

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. **Fact.** The Standard Savings minimum was ₹5,000 on July 15 under SV-2. “The minimum is ₹5,000” is a reference answer. A ₹10,000 claim receives FAIL.
2. **Equivalent meaning.** Both require at least ₹5,000 for the same account. The reference is a reviewed way to say it. An exact match can miss equally valid wording.
3. **SV-2.** It is approved and effective for that date. The draft's later edit time carries no policy authority.
4. **It separates below from at or below.** A program that accidentally includes equality can pass easier cases above and below the threshold and still fail this boundary.
5. **PASS for the reviewed abstention, NEEDS REVIEW for the conflict.** The bounded ATM case has known expected behavior. The waiver claim lacks a resolved source of truth.
6. **A keyword test can find the amount without checking its frequency.** The reply’s policy claim converts a monthly charge to a daily charge. Review the relationship between the amount and the frequency.
7. **The score could exaggerate performance on fresh questions.** The assistant may have seen the evaluation answers during tuning. Use held-out, independently reviewed cases and check for near-duplicates.
8. **Rerun both assistant versions on the same corrected benchmark.** Preserve the older reports with their version IDs. A change in labels and case coverage can change a score even when the assistant's behavior stays the same.

## Common Mistakes

- **Selecting the newest file automatically.** Select by approval, scope, and the case's as-of date. Use the register to check supersession.
- **Treating every unfamiliar reply as a failure.** Separate reviewed errors from replies you have yet to judge. A paraphrase can be correct.
- **Guessing at a policy conflict.** Keep the source IDs and ask the policy owner for a documented decision.
- **Using a keyword as the entire rubric.** Check what the reply claims, especially thresholds, units, frequency, and negation.
- **Editing a previous result in place.** Keep the original report, issue a documented benchmark revision, and run comparisons on aligned versions.
