## Why This Lesson Matters

Imagine you ordered a jacket online from Harbor Leaf Market. It arrived 20 days ago, and you haven't worn it. You type “Can I get my money back?” The help page calls this “requesting a refund.” A person reading both phrases can see what you mean. A computer that only counts matching words has less to go on.

Then the team sees “You may request a refund within 90 days of delivery.” It is nearly a word-for-word copy of the approved answer, except the store allows 30 calendar days, including day 30. A customer on day 45 would get the wrong impression from the 90-day reply.

The store, policy, replies, and numerical vectors in this lesson are fictional. The store accepts refund requests for unused online purchases within 30 calendar days of delivery. The question we score is about that deadline for an eligible item. From [Ground truth, references and benchmarks](#m02-t02), bring an approved source for each fact. ## Beginner Explanation

### Start with the words you can see

Put “You may request a refund within 30 days of delivery” above “Ask for your money back up to thirty calendar days after it arrives.” With case and punctuation set aside, the lines share just “days.” Read again without counting. “Money back” means a refund here, “arrives” marks delivery, and thirty is 30. Both give the same instruction for the same eligible purchase. A restatement that preserves the meaning is a **paraphrase**. Comparing the meanings rather than the written words is called **semantic similarity**.

Watch what happens with “a refund is available during the first month.” It sounds close, but “first month” can be read in more than one way. A calendar month can have more or fewer than 30 days. When the deadline matters, send this wording for clarification. Being about the same event is a weaker claim than preserving the rule.

### Give the computer something it can compare

Mark a starting point on a sheet of paper. Move three squares right and one up, then draw an arrow to that spot. Write the directions as `(3, 1)`. Swap them to `(1, 3)` and the arrow turns. This ordered group of numbers is a **vector**, and each number is a **component**. You needed two directions to draw the arrow, so the vector has two **dimensions**. A model can keep track of more dimensions than you can draw.

For the demonstration, imagine that someone has already placed the approved reply at `(4, 0)` and the paraphrase at `(3, 1)`. These are hand-authored positions. An **embedding** is a numerical representation made by a model for text or another input. A sentence encoder is a model that takes text in and returns a vector. A **pretrained encoder** has already learned from examples before you use it here. Depending on what it learned, sentences about similar things can end up pointing in similar directions. [Sentence-BERT research paper](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1410/)

Real encoders often return many numbers so they can represent more patterns than our two-position sketch. You generally cannot point to one learned position and call it “refund” or “truth.” The positions work together. Even in this lesson we leave the two teaching positions unnamed. An evaluator compares the whole vectors and checks how well the scores work on reviewed examples.

### Compare the directions

Draw an arrow from the origin, where both coordinates are zero, to `(4, 0)`. Now draw one to `(8, 0)`. The second goes twice as far, but both point straight right. The length of an arrow is its **magnitude**. If you want to know whether arrows point the same way, their lengths alone won't help.

Try a third arrow to `(3, 1)`. Multiply the numbers in matching positions and add the results. With `(4, 0)`, that gives `(4 × 3) + (0 × 1) = 12`. This is the **dot product**. Doubling one arrow doubles that number even when its direction stays fixed.

To account for length, find the magnitude of each arrow. Square each component by multiplying it by itself, add the squares, then take the square root. The square root of 9 is 3 because 3 × 3 = 9. The reference length is √(16 + 0) = 4. The paraphrase length is √(9 + 1) = √10. Divide 12 by `4 × √10` and you get about 0.949. This is **cosine similarity**, the dot product divided by the product of the two magnitudes. It compares the angle between the arrows. [SciPy cosine distance definition](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.spatial.distance.cosine.html)

Cosine runs from −1 to 1 for nonzero vectors. A score of 1 means the same direction, 0 means a right angle, and −1 means opposite directions. These are directions in the model’s numbers. A score of −1 does not label a contradiction, and 0.949 does not mean a 94.9% chance the reply is correct. A vector of all zeros has no direction, so dividing by its length would be undefined. Record the score as unavailable. Also treat unequal vector lengths or vectors from different encoders as a setup problem, even when the scores would be convenient to compare.

### Check the parts a score can miss

Read these three lines against the store's rule.

- “Ask for your money back up to thirty calendar days after it arrives” preserves the refund request, deadline, and starting event.
- “You may request a refund within 90 days of delivery” changes the deadline. The topic remains the same and the numerical fact is wrong.
- “You cannot request a refund within 30 days of delivery” keeps the number and reverses the permission. A small word changes whether the rest of the statement is allowed. That reversal is **negation**. Here it creates a **contradiction** with the policy.

“You may request a refund” is also about the right topic, but leaves the customer without a deadline. A related statement can be incomplete, wrong, or opposite in meaning. Another useful question is whether the policy actually guarantees the claim. That relationship is called **entailment**. The right to request a refund does not guarantee it will be approved. Cosine gives a direction comparison. Check the approved policy for facts, dates, conditions, and whether the reply says enough to answer the question.

You can implement some factual checks as direct rules. For example, once the text has been reliably read as allowing a request within 90 days of delivery, 90 and 30 can be compared with an exact number check. Confirm the event and the unit before comparing the numbers. If the reply says “month,” mixes two deadlines, or leaves out the event, ask a reviewer to inspect the original text. The lesson's code is given reviewed facts as inputs. It does not extract them from a sentence.

### Choose which replies need attention

Suppose you have ten replies and enough time to read only a few right away. You can use the score to sort replies into a likely-match group and a group for closer reading. That first sorting step is called **triage**. A number you use to divide the groups is a **threshold**. Here we shortlist when the unrounded cosine is at least the threshold. Policy mismatches can still block a reply before that shortlist is used.

To pick a threshold, start with a labeled practice set. The labels come from people who read the approved source and reply. Try more than one cutoff. Then test both on a separate, **held-out** set that wasn't used to choose those cutoffs. Choosing a cutoff for the task using labeled examples is **threshold calibration**. If you choose a new cutoff after seeing the held-out results, get a fresh set for the next independent check.

For a small development example, imagine four replies scored 0.96 (equivalent), 0.85 (equivalent), 0.93 (wrong deadline), and 0.84 (missing deadline). A cutoff of 0.90 shortlists two replies, one correct and one wrong, and misses one valid reply. A cutoff of 0.80 shortlists all four. It recovers the valid reply and adds one more false shortlist. A **false shortlist** here means a reply the score shortlisted that the reviewer did not consider equivalent. A **missed match** is an equivalent reply the score left out. The lab tests these same two preset cutoffs on a different set. Do not treat either number as a universal standard.

When using real embeddings, save the encoder name and exact version, how you prepared the text, the reference wording, the scores or vectors, and the threshold alongside your labels. If the encoder changes, encode both sets of texts with the new encoder and compare the two scoring setups on the same reviewed cases. For example, a new encoder could give the same paraphrase 0.84 where the old one gave 0.93. Test both versions before reusing the old cutoff. An equal number of components does not make vectors from two models comparable. Keep actual failed requests, which produced no reply, in a separate count so they neither earn zero similarity nor inflate the number of wrong answers.

## Worked Demonstration

### Example 1. A paraphrase loses on word overlap

**User question.** How long after delivery can I ask for a refund on my unused online purchase?

**Approved reference.** “You may request a refund within 30 days of delivery.” The policy counts calendar days and includes day 30.

**Actual LLM response.** The fictional saved reply says “Ask for your money back up to thirty calendar days after it arrives.”

**Assessment.** Lowercase the two lines, remove the final periods, and cross off the words they share. Only “days” matches, so the reference coverage is 1 ÷ 10 = 0.10. The supplied teaching vectors are `(4, 0)` and `(3, 1)`. Their cosine is 12 ÷ (4 × √10), about 0.949. Next check the text itself. “Ask for” preserves the permission, “thirty calendar days” preserves the deadline, and “after it arrives” preserves the starting event.

**Failure classification.** The reply fits the policy, so a rule that rejects it for low word overlap would make a mistake. Use this pair to test whether a new scoring setup can find equivalent wording. The invented cosine explains how the approach could help. It gives no measured result for a real encoder.

### Example 2. Compare direction before comparing length

**User question.** Would a longer vector make a candidate seem more similar to the approved refund reply?

**Approved reference.** Keep the same reply and its teaching vector `(4, 0)`. Compare only the numbers in this example.

**Actual LLM response.** Use a fictional measurement for the same saved line before and after scaling. Its vector changes from `(3, 1)` to `(6, 2)`. This isolates length from changes in the reply.

**Assessment.** The dot product goes from 4 × 3 + 0 × 1 = 12 to 4 × 6 + 0 × 2 = 24. The original length is √10. The second is √(36 + 4) = √40 = 2 × √10. The reference length stays 4. The new cosine is 24 ÷ (4 × 2 × √10), which is the original 0.949. If you instead turn the candidate arrow up to `(0, 3)`, the dot product becomes zero and cosine becomes 0.

**Failure classification.** Ranking these two scaled copies by dot product alone would reward a longer arrow even though it points the same way. The angle comparison avoids that effect. This deliberate scaling test does not tell you why a real encoder might produce a longer vector.

### Example 3. The wrong deadline leads the ranking

**User question.** On day 45 after delivery, can I still request a refund for an eligible item under the standard policy?

**Approved reference.** Use the same 30-day policy statement as evidence. Since 45 is greater than 30, the ordinary request window has closed.

**Actual LLM response.** One fictional reply restates the correct rule as “Ask for your money back up to thirty calendar days after it arrives.” A second says “You may request a refund within 90 days of delivery.”

**Assessment.** The paraphrase scores 12 ÷ (4 × √10) ≈ 0.949. The 90-day reply uses the supplied vector `(4, 1)` and scores 16 ÷ (4 × √17) ≈ 0.970. Both clear a cutoff of 0.90. Now put the customer's day 45 next to the two deadlines. It is past day 30, but still inside day 90. Following the higher-scoring reply would lead this customer to think the window was still open.

**Failure classification.** Block the 90-day reply for a policy mismatch. The paraphrase preserves the standard deadline, although a direct “the window has closed” would answer this customer's specific question more completely. Keep the refund deadline check even if the similarity ranking looks strong across the rest of the set.

### Step 1 — Count the words before checking what they mean

Measure how many distinct words in the approved line reappear in two drafts. `lower()` makes the letters lowercase. `rstrip(".")` removes final periods, and `split()` breaks the line at whitespace. `set()` keeps one copy of each distinct word. `&` keeps only the words in both sets. This coverage measure uses the number of distinct reference words as its denominator.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
def words(text):
    return set(text.lower().rstrip(".").split())

reference = "You may request a refund within 30 days of delivery."
paraphrase = "Ask for your money back up to thirty calendar days after it arrives."
wrong = "You may request a refund within 90 days of delivery."
reference_words = words(reference)

for label, reply in [("Paraphrase", paraphrase), ("Wrong deadline", wrong)]:
    shared = len(reference_words & words(reply))
    print(f"{label}: {shared} of {len(reference_words)} reference words")
```

```text
Wrong deadline: 9 of 10 reference words
```

### Step 4 — Compare direction and handle a missing score

Multiply corresponding components to get the dot product. `zip(left, right)` supplies pairs of corresponding components, and `sum(a * b for a, b in ...)` adds their products. `hypot(*left)` passes the separate components to Python's length function. `None` represents a missing result. The check for different lengths keeps `zip()` from quietly dropping an unmatched component.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from math import hypot

def compare(left, right):
    if len(left) != len(right):
        return None
    product = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(left, right))
    lengths = hypot(*left) * hypot(*right)
    if lengths == 0:
        return None
    return product, product / lengths

reference = (4, 0)
candidate = (3, 4)
dot, score = compare(reference, candidate)
print(f"Dot product: {dot}")
print(f"Cosine: {score:.3f}")
if compare(reference, (0, 0)) is None:
    print("Zero vector: unavailable")
```

```text
Dot product: 12
Cosine: 0.600
Zero vector: unavailable
```

**Experiment.** Change `candidate` to `(0, 5)`. Its length is still 5. Leave the separate zero-vector check alone.

```text

Cosine: 0.000
Zero vector: unavailable
```

The reference arrow points right and the changed candidate points straight up. Both still have a length, so the division is `0 ÷ (4 × 5) = 0`. **Checkpoint.** Why does the last line still say “unavailable”? Its separate test uses `(0, 0)`, an arrow with no length at all. That would require dividing by zero, so the function returns no score. Keep these outcomes separate when counting low scores.

### Step 5 — Rank saved replies and read the policy labels

Score three fictional refund replies with the same reference vector. A asks for money back within thirty days, B changes the window to 90 days, and C says “From delivery, you have 30 days to ask for your money back.” The reviewed policy judgment is already attached to each reply. The list `ranked` collects a tuple with the score first. `sort(reverse=True)` orders those tuples from highest to lowest, comparing the score before the other fields. There are no tied scores in this exercise. `:.3f` displays three decimal places.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from math import hypot

def cosine(left, right):
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(left, right)) / (hypot(*left) * hypot(*right))

reference = (4, 0)
replies = [
    ("A", (3, 1), "correct paraphrase"),
    ("B", (4, 1), "wrong deadline"),
    ("C", (4, 3), "correct reordering"),
]
ranked = []
for name, vector, policy_label in replies:
    ranked.append((cosine(reference, vector), name, policy_label))

ranked.sort(reverse=True)
for score, name, policy_label in ranked:
    print(f"{name}: {score:.3f} | {policy_label}")
```

```text
B: 0.970 | wrong deadline
A: 0.949 | correct paraphrase
C: 0.800 | correct reordering
```


C's arrow now points closer to the reference direction, so its rank rises. It is still the same reply. **Checkpoint.** Has the wrong deadline improved? B is unchanged. ### Step 7 — Try two thresholds on held-out refund replies

The refund team chose 0.90 and 0.80 using the earlier practice records. This program uses a different collection of frozen replies to compare what those choices would do. The team already read each reply and recorded whether it was equivalent to the approved answer. It also wrote down the day count and whether the reply allows or denies the request. Treat those supplied facts and labels as separate evidence. The toy vectors cannot supply either of them. Think of the rows as a saved snapshot from the scoring stage: you can compare the near misses again without regenerating the numbers. In a real experiment the snapshot would contain cached outputs from a named encoder. Here it contains hand-authored teaching values.

The approved reference is “You may request a refund within 30 days of delivery.” Each record holds the case ID, the captured text, the teaching vector, the reviewed deadline in calendar days from delivery, the permission, and the equivalence label. If the trigger or units are unclear, the reviewer leaves the deadline missing. A request that produced no reply has `None` in its reply field. If a saved reply has no vector, the cosine function returns a missing result. The program uses `continue` to skip the rest of the loop for that record. `score is not None and ...` checks that a score exists before comparing it with a number. A missing score stays out of the shortlist counts, while a known policy mismatch can still block the reply. In `above = score >= threshold`, the result is `True` when the score meets or exceeds the cutoff. `+= 1` adds one to a count. The route `CHECKED MATCH` means the supplied policy facts matched and the cosine met this illustrative cutoff.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from math import hypot

def cosine(left, right):
    if right is None or len(left) != len(right):
        return None
    lengths = hypot(*left) * hypot(*right)
    if lengths == 0:
        return None
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(left, right)) / lengths

reference = (4, 0)
policy_days = 30
threshold = 0.90
cases = [
    ("H1", "Ask for your money back up to thirty calendar days after it arrives.",
     (3, 1), 30, "allows", True),
    ("H2", "You may request a refund within 90 days of delivery.",
     (4, 1), 90, "allows", False),
    ("H3", "You cannot request a refund within 30 days of delivery.",
     (4, -1), 30, "denies", False),
    ("H4", "From delivery, you have 30 days to ask for your money back.",
     (4, 3), 30, "allows", True),
    ("H5", "You may request a refund.",
     (3, 2), None, "allows", False),
    ("H6", None, None, None, None, None),
    ("H7", "Track the parcel in Orders.",
     (3, 4), None, None, False),
]

scored = shortlisted = false_shortlist = missed = no_reply = 0
print(f"Threshold: {threshold:.2f}")
for case_id, reply, vector, days, permission, equivalent in cases:
    if reply is None:
        no_reply += 1
        print(f"{case_id}: no reply | REQUEST ERROR")
        continue

    score = cosine(reference, vector)
    above = score is not None and score >= threshold
    score_text = "unavailable"
    if score is not None:
        score_text = f"{score:.3f}"
        scored += 1
        if above:
            shortlisted += 1
            if not equivalent:
                false_shortlist += 1
        elif equivalent:
            missed += 1

    if permission == "denies":
        route = "BLOCK (permission)"
    elif days is None or permission != "allows":
        route = "REVIEW (missing facts)"
    elif days != policy_days:
        route = f"BLOCK (expected {policy_days}, found {days})"
    elif score is None:
        route = "REVIEW (vector)"
    elif above:
        route = "CHECKED MATCH"
    else:
        route = "REVIEW (low cosine)"
    print(f"{case_id}: {score_text} | {route}")

print(f"Score only: {shortlisted}/{scored} shortlisted | "
      f"false shortlists: {false_shortlist} | missed matches: {missed}")
print(f"Request errors: {no_reply}")
```

```text
Threshold: 0.90
H1: 0.949 | CHECKED MATCH
H2: 0.970 | BLOCK (expected 30, found 90)
H3: 0.970 | BLOCK (permission)
H4: 0.800 | REVIEW (low cosine)
H5: 0.832 | REVIEW (missing facts)
H6: no reply | REQUEST ERROR
H7: 0.600 | REVIEW (missing facts)
Score only: 3/6 shortlisted | false shortlists: 2 | missed matches: 1
Request errors: 1
```

**Experiment.** Change only `threshold = 0.90` to `threshold = 0.80`. Keep the saved text, approved facts, vectors, and reviewer labels fixed.

```text

H1: 0.949 | CHECKED MATCH
H2: 0.970 | BLOCK (expected 30, found 90)
H3: 0.970 | BLOCK (permission)
H4: 0.800 | CHECKED MATCH
H5: 0.832 | REVIEW (missing facts)
```

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. Equal length gives the same number of positions, but the positions were learned in different models. Score complete pairs with each encoder separately, then compare where their rankings and decisions differ on the same reviewed examples.
2. The first component stays at 3 and the second rises from 1 to 3. The arrow turns upward. There are still two component positions, since the repeated value 3 occupies both positions. The lesson assigns neither component a meaning such as “refund.”
3. Doubling the candidate doubles each matching-position product and therefore their sum. Its magnitude doubles too, so the cosine ratio remains the same.
4. Square 0 and −5 to get 0 and 25. Their sum is 25 and its square root is 5. Squaring makes the contribution nonnegative.
5. The right-angle pair has valid lengths and a dot product of zero, so its cosine is zero. The all-zero vector makes the denominator zero and leaves the score undefined.
6. Confirm the reply and approved evidence were held fixed, and score both texts with each version. Reread the paraphrase against the policy. The score may reflect the change of encoder rather than a change in the text or its correctness.

## Common Mistakes

A large score can make it tempting to clear a reply immediately. Read the deadline, permission, and event first. If you cannot verify a fact from the text and the approved source, route it to review.

Avoid comparing scores from different model versions as if the vectors shared one set of directions. Freeze and record the settings. When a setup changes, score a common set of reference and reply pairs with each version.

Count failures to capture a reply separately. A missing response cannot tell you whether two answers use similar wording or preserve the same rule. Giving it a score of zero would mix operational reliability with text comparison.
