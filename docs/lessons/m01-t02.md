## Why This Lesson Matters

Imagine asking two people at a bank, “How much do I need to keep in this account?” One says, “The minimum is ₹5,000.” The other says, “Keep at least five thousand rupees in your account.” You heard two different answers that mean the same thing. You’ll learn to answer these questions by seeing how a reply is built, then keeping a small record of repeated attempts. The examples use Harbor Leaf Bank, a fictional bank. ## Beginner Explanation

### 1. Watch a reply take shape

Think of the suggestions that appear as you type on a phone. After “The minimum,” you might see “is” or “balance.” Once you add “is,” the next suggestions change. That gives you a way to picture how a model writes a reply.

When you ask Harbor Leaf’s assistant about the minimum, the app sends the question along with its instructions and the relevant bank policy. We’ll call that whole set of information the **prompt**. Using the model to write a reply from that prompt is called **inference**. The model has already been adjusted using examples in an earlier stage called **training**. Asking it a question here uses the model as it is.

As you saw in the previous chapter, a **token** is a piece of text the model represents as a number. Say the reply so far is “The minimum.” For our sketch, imagine that “ is” and “ balance” are two possible next pieces. The model reads the prompt and the reply so far and scores the possible next pieces. Those scores can be turned into chances for a draw. A chance expressed as a number, such as 60 out of 100, is a **probability**.

Now add “ is.” A number could make sense next. If you add “ balance” instead, you might need “ is” before the number. Try finishing both versions aloud. Each chosen piece becomes part of what the model uses to score the next one. That’s why a small change early in a reply can lead to a different sentence. The Python activities use prewritten pieces and invented chances. A real model works out new scores at every step, and its tokenizer may split the text differently.

### 2. Choose the most likely piece or draw from several

Imagine a bag with 60 blue counters, 30 green ones, and 10 white ones. If you’re allowed to pick whichever color appears most often, you’ll choose blue every time while the bag stays the same. If you reach in without looking, blue is more likely than either other color, and you can still draw green or white.

Picking the highest-scoring next token is called **greedy selection**. Drawing a token according to its chance is called **sampling**. You can think of the chances as the proportions of colored counters. The model makes a decision for each next token. Greedy selection chooses the locally highest score. It doesn’t check every possible finished reply or verify the bank’s policy.

In the lab, we’ll use “is,” “balance,” and “required” as our three possible next pieces, with invented chances of 60%, 30%, and 10%. Picture putting each counter back before the next draw. The bag stays the same, yet you can draw blue, then green. A model can also make a different sampled choice when you send the same prompt again. Four attempts tell you what happened in those four draws. You’d need many more to get a dependable estimate of how often each choice appears.

### 3. Change how much the choices differ

Suppose the bank wants the assistant’s wording to vary less. Go back to the bag with 60 blue, 30 green, and 10 white counters. Picture a second bag where blue has an even larger share. You’ll draw blue more often, but green and white can still come out as long as some of each remain.

The model has a setting called **temperature** that can make a similar change to the chances before it draws. A lower positive temperature gives the stronger choices a bigger lead. A higher temperature brings the chances closer together. Temperature 1 leaves the original chances as they were. Some services use 0 to ask for greedy selection. Check how the service you’re testing handles that setting.

You can see the effect by trying two numbers in Step 3. At temperature 1, “is” has 60% of the chance. At 0.5, it has about 78%. The other pieces still have some chance left, so two draws can still come out differently. The Python step will walk you through how it got those numbers. If a real service uses greedy selection, changes in the model or in how the service does its calculations can still affect which piece wins. Check repeated replies before promising they will match.

Would the higher chance make a bank answer more accurate? Ask which choice got the extra chance. If the preferred amount is wrong, making it more likely can repeat the error. Review the claims against the policy before choosing settings for a customer-facing use.

### 4. Decide how many likely choices to keep

What if the bank wants to leave the least likely piece out of the draw entirely? Lay out 60 blue, 30 green, and 10 white counters in groups. Put the groups back into an empty bag, biggest first. Stop once the bag holds at least 80 counters. Blue alone gives you 60. With green, you have 90, so the white group stays out.

The model can use a setting called **top-p** to do this with the chances for the next piece. A top-p of 0.8 means “keep adding whole choices from most likely to least likely until you’ve kept at least 80% of the original chance.” This method is also called **nucleus sampling**. The choice that takes the sum over the line stays in the draw. Different services can handle equal chances differently. Our three chances are all different.

With 90 counters in the bag, blue now has 60 chances out of 90, about 66.7%, and green has 30 out of 90, about 33.3%. Try a cutoff of 95 on paper. You’ll need all three groups. For the model, the same cutoff may keep two choices at one point in a reply and more at another, depending on the chances at that point. If you’re checking why a piece became less common or disappeared, change only temperature or only top-p and compare what happened.

### 5. Repeat a draw and record what was held fixed

Imagine two friends have copies of the same shuffled deck and turn over four cards from the top. They’ll see the same sequence. If one uses a different shuffle, the sequence may change, even though the decks contain the same cards.

Python can do something like that with numbers. It has a program that produces numbers that look random, but follows a fixed recipe. It's called a **pseudorandom number generator**. The number you give it to get started is called a **seed**. In Step 5, starting two fresh generators with seed 7 gives you the same first four draws. Asking one generator for a fifth draw continues where it left off.

Suppose the bank tries the same request four times. Do the words match? Does the minimum balance stay correct each time? Say which result you’re checking before you count. That’s how you study **repeatability**, or how consistently you get the same result under the conditions you tested. A real service may let you set a seed. It can help, but the service may still vary in how it runs the model. Save the seed along with the request, settings, and model version so someone else can see what you held fixed.

A **model snapshot** is a specific published version of a model, where the provider makes one available. A general name may point to a different version later. If you compare March’s results with today’s and you saved only the general name, you may have changed the model without realizing it. Record the snapshot or other version information you actually have, and flag what’s unknown. A version ID makes a comparison easier to explain. Other parts of the request may still have changed.

### 6. See why a reply ended

You ask a colleague to give a short announcement. They might finish the sentence, stop at an agreed cue, or get cut off when time runs out. In every case you stop hearing words, but the reason tells you what to do next.

**Maximum output tokens** cap how many tokens the model may generate for a request. If it uses the entire allowance and could have continued, you may see a length-related ending. Suppose “The minimum is” is all that fits. You received words, but the customer still needs the amount.

A **stop condition** is any rule that ends generation. It might be the output limit, a special end marker generated by the model, or a configured text sequence. Different interfaces may report stop reasons or hide markers differently. This lab uses the classroom marker “<END>” to signal an intended end. The program doesn’t show that marker in the reply.

A **timeout** means part of the system waited longer than allowed and ended the attempt. A timeout with no reply gives you no amount to check. If an answer arrives and claims ₹10,000, that’s a different result. Save what ended the attempt, then check any usable answer against the policy. If the result doesn’t tell you why it ended, record that the reason is unknown.

### 7. Work out what changed

Suppose a customer shows you one reply that says “five thousand rupees” and another that says “₹10,000.” Before you touch a setting, check that both customers asked about Standard Savings and both requests included the same rule. Maybe the app sent a different policy excerpt on the second try. The chat screen alone won’t tell you.

To test a temperature change, keep the actual questions, instructions, policy, model version, and rules for judging the replies the same. Hold top-p, the output cap, and any stop settings fixed too. If you can set a seed, plan which seeds to use for repeated attempts and use the same list for both temperatures. This lets you compare several attempts without basing the entire comparison on one draw.

Run the original setup several times to see how much the replies already vary. Then try both temperatures the same number of times on the same questions. In a live test, mix the order. If you run all the low-temperature attempts in the morning and all the high-temperature attempts later, a difference in how busy the service is could affect the results.

For each attempt, save the prompt the model received, the version and settings you know, the run order, the reply or error, and why the attempt ended. Mark any detail you couldn’t get as unknown. Check whether each usable reply gets the policy right, and count incomplete or missing replies alongside those judgments. Report what you saw in the test. A few attempts on one question can give you a lead to follow up.

### Step 1 — Build the reply one piece at a time

Before we draw any pieces, let’s see what happens if the choices are already saved. You’ll build a reply from left to right so you can see exactly when the amount appears.

A **string** is text inside quotation marks. A **variable** is a name you give a value. The single equals sign `=` saves the value on the right under the name on the left. `reply = ""` starts the reply as an empty string. The chosen pieces are in a **list**, a series of items in square brackets with commas between them. We’re treating each item as one token in this exercise.

Read `for piece in pieces:` as “take each piece in order and call this one `piece`.” The colon starts instructions for the loop. The spaces on the left, called **indentation**, show which lines to repeat. Here `+` joins two strings, and `print()` shows the growing reply. With two items inside its parentheses, separated by a comma, Python displays them with a space between them. Spaces inside a string stay part of that string.

**Copy and run.** Paste the program into your editor and run it. Look for the first line where a customer could read the amount.

```python
pieces = ["The", " minimum", " is", " ₹5,000", "."]
reply = ""

for piece in pieces:
    reply = reply + piece
    print("So far:", reply)
```

### Step 3 — Adjust the chances with temperature

You’ll now change the chances before drawing. Start from the 60%, 30%, and 10% you already know. At temperature 0.5, see how much of the chance moves to “is.”

Start with the first chance, written as `0.60` for 60%. In `1 / temperature`, the slash divides. At temperature 0.5, the result is 2. Python uses `**` for powers, so `0.60 ** 2` means multiply 0.60 by itself. This gives 0.36. The other two choices give 0.09 and 0.01. Call these new numbers **weights**. They show how much of the draw each choice gets relative to the others.

Start with `weights = []`, an empty list. Each time the first loop calculates a weight, `weights.append(...)` adds it to the end of that list. The function `sum(weights)` adds the three weights to get 0.46. The second loop turns each weight back into a chance by dividing by that total. For the first piece, 0.36 out of 0.46 is about 78.3%.

In the print line, an `f` before a quoted string lets you put values into it using braces. In `{chance:.1%}`, `.1%` tells Python to show a fraction as a percentage with one digit after the decimal point. It rounds each line for display. The full numbers can sum to 100% even if the individually rounded lines don’t.

**Copy and run.** Paste this program and run it. Compare the first printed chance with its original 60%.

```python
temperature = 0.5
original = [0.60, 0.30, 0.10]
weights = []

for chance in original:
    weights.append(chance ** (1 / temperature))

total = sum(weights)
print("Temperature:", temperature)
for weight in weights:
    chance = weight / total
    print(f"Chance: {chance:.1%}")
```

**Expected output.** The strongest piece is now much more likely. The weakest choice still has a chance.

```text
Temperature: 0.5
Chance: 78.3%
Chance: 19.6%
Chance: 2.2%
```

**Experiment.** Change `temperature` to `1.0` and run again. The exponent is now 1, so each weight stays the same. Their total is already 1.

```text
Temperature: 1.0
Chance: 60.0%
Chance: 30.0%
Chance: 10.0%
```

**Checkpoint.** Has the program found the best setting for the bank? It has shown how one setting changes the chances for one invented list of choices. To recommend a setting, you’d need to look at the meaning of the replies and test more questions. This snippet requires a positive temperature because it divides by that value.

### Step 4 — Keep enough choices to reach a cutoff

Try setting aside the least likely choices before you draw. We’ll use whole percentage points so you can add them on paper. This is top-p with a cutoff written as 80 out of 100 rather than 0.8 out of 1.

Each pair inside parentheses is a **tuple**, two values kept together in order here. The list is already sorted from most likely to least likely. `for word, points in choices:` takes the two values from one pair and gives them their own names. This is called **unpacking**. The loop keeps the pair, then adds its points. When `>=` (“at least”) becomes true, `break` ends the loop. The pair that reached the cutoff has already been kept.

The final loop divides each kept entry’s points by the total kept. This is what gives you chances that sum to 100% across the smaller set.

**Copy and run.** Paste the block and run it. Predict how many choices you’ll keep before the 80-point cutoff is reached.

```python
cutoff = 80
choices = [("is", 60), ("balance", 30), ("required", 10)]
kept = []
covered = 0

for word, points in choices:
    kept.append((word, points))
    covered = covered + points
    if covered >= cutoff:
        break

print("Covered points:", covered)
for word, points in kept:
    chance = points / covered
    print(f"{word}: {chance:.1%}")
```

**Expected output.** The first choice gives you 60 points. Adding “balance” takes you to 90. The final 10-point choice stays outside the set.

```text
Covered points: 90
is: 66.7%
balance: 33.3%
```

**Experiment.** Set `cutoff` to `95` and run again. The first two choices cover too little, so the third one stays in. With all 100 points kept, each final chance matches its original share.

```text
Covered points: 100
is: 60.0%
balance: 30.0%
required: 10.0%
```

**Checkpoint.** At a cutoff of 80, why did we cover 90 points? We kept whole choices. The second choice takes the running total past 80, and the rule keeps that choice in full.

### Step 5 — Repeat a set of draws with a seed

Now let Python supply the draw positions. To see what a seed can control, start two fresh number generators with the same seed and use each for four draws. Think of these as four separate attempts at picking the next piece after “The minimum.” The starting text and chances reset for each attempt. The generator keeps moving through its number sequence.

The `import random` line makes Python’s built-in tools for simulated draws available. `random.Random(seed)` creates a fresh generator. Each `generator.random()` gets its next number. The smallest possible value is zero, and the value is always below 1. Multiply by 100 with `*`, then use `int()` to drop the decimal part. For a number such as 0.327, those steps give 32.7 and then position 32. The tests use the same ranges as Step 2.

A **function** is a named block you can run again. `def show_draws(seed):` names the work and gives it a name, `seed`, for its input. The indented lines run each time you call the function. `range(4)` supplies four positions, starting at zero. The name `turn` marks which repetition you’re on. The print line adds one so the report starts at 1. Calling `show_draws()` with a value starts the function and creates the generator inside it again.

**Copy and run.** Paste the program and run it. Compare the two sets line by line.

```python
import random


def show_draws(seed):
    generator = random.Random(seed)
    print("Seed:", seed)
    for turn in range(4):
        draw = int(generator.random() * 100)
        if draw < 60:
            piece = "is"
        elif draw < 90:
            piece = "balance"
        else:
            piece = "required"
        print(turn + 1, draw, piece)


second_seed = 7
show_draws(7)
show_draws(second_seed)
```

**Expected output.** Both sets start at seed 7 and do the same work in the same order. The small draw can repeat a choice several times.

```text

1 32 is
2 15 is
3 65 balance
4 7 is
Seed: 7
1 32 is
2 15 is
3 65 balance
4 7 is
```


### Step 6 — Record why the reply stopped

Revisit the saved pieces from Step 1. This time add a classroom end marker and a maximum output length. Two runs can end for different reasons, and you want that reason beside the reply before a reviewer checks it.

`==` asks whether two values are equal. In the first check, it compares the current piece with the marker. A match sets the stop reason and `break` leaves the loop before the marker is added. A normal piece gets joined to the reply and `used` goes up by one. If `used` equals the budget, the second check records a length stop and leaves the loop. If the saved list ends without either kind of stop, the reason stays “saved stream ended.” This toy counter uses a positive budget and counts only visible reply pieces.

**Copy and run.** Paste the program and run it. Look at the reason and then at the text the customer received.

```python
budget = 8
pieces = ["The", " minimum", " is", " ₹5,000", ".", "<END>"]
reply = ""
used = 0
reason = "saved stream ended"

for piece in pieces:
    if piece == "<END>":
        reason = "end marker"
        break
    reply = reply + piece
    used = used + 1
    if used == budget:
        reason = "length limit"
        break

print("Reply:", reply)
print("Visible pieces:", used)
print("Stop reason:", reason)
```

**Expected output.** The saved marker arrived with room left. The customer sees the completed sentence without the marker.

```text
Reply: The minimum is ₹5,000.
Visible pieces: 5
Stop reason: end marker
```

**Experiment.** Set `budget` to `3` and rerun. The third visible piece uses the last available slot, so the amount and marker never get read.

```text

Visible pieces: 3
Stop reason: length limit
```

**Checkpoint.** Should a reviewer label the second reply “wrong amount”? There is no amount to compare with the policy. Record an incomplete answer and investigate whether the output allowance was large enough. ### Step 7 — Compare repeated attempts and keep every outcome visible

You’re helping the bank decide what to test next. The program reads the eight saved attempts from Example 3. It shows how often customers got the right amount and how many replies contained an amount you could judge. Both counts matter when some attempts end early or return no text.

Read each row as a tuple with five entries. In order, they are temperature, seed, stop reason, reply, and policy review. `None` means a value is absent. On a timeout there is no reply or policy review. The cut-off reply is saved, but it’s missing the amount, so it has no policy review label in this exercise. The other policy labels were assigned by a human who checked the savings policy.

A loop can sit inside another loop. For each temperature, set the counts back to zero. Then read every saved row. `!=` means “is different from.” If the row belongs to the other temperature, `continue` skips to the next row.

Read the stop reason first. For a timeout, print “no reply.” For the saved length stop, print the partial text. For a finished reply, look up its saved review label, either PASS or WRONG AMOUNT. A vertical bar inside quotes is just a separator in the report. After the inner loop, the program prints both totals for the current temperature. It uses the supplied labels and stop reasons. It doesn’t decide for itself whether the words mean ₹5,000.

**Copy and run.** Paste the whole program and run it. For each temperature, find the total number of attempts and the number of replies with enough information to judge.

```python
snapshot = "harbor-demo-v1"
top_p = 1.0
budget = 12
direct = "The minimum is ₹5,000."
alternate = "Keep at least five thousand rupees in the account."
wrong = "The minimum is ₹10,000."
partial = "For Standard Savings, the minimum balance you need to maintain is"

runs = [
    (0.2, 7, "finished", direct, "PASS"),
    (0.2, 8, "timeout", None, None),
    (0.2, 9, "finished", alternate, "PASS"),
    (0.2, 10, "finished", direct, "PASS"),
    (0.8, 7, "finished", alternate, "PASS"),
    (0.8, 8, "finished", wrong, "WRONG AMOUNT"),
    (0.8, 9, "length limit", partial, None),
    (0.8, 10, "finished", direct, "PASS"),
]

print("Snapshot:", snapshot, "| Top-p:", top_p, "| Output cap:", budget)
print("Configured stop text: none")

for temperature in [0.2, 0.8]:
    attempts = 0
    correct = 0
    wrong_amount = 0
    incomplete = 0
    timeouts = 0
    print("Temperature:", temperature)

    for setting, seed, stop_reason, reply, review in runs:
        if setting != temperature:
            continue

        attempts = attempts + 1
        if stop_reason == "timeout":
            timeouts = timeouts + 1
            print("Seed:", seed, "| timeout | no reply")
        elif stop_reason == "length limit":
            incomplete = incomplete + 1
            print("Seed:", seed, "| incomplete |", reply)
        else:
            if review == "PASS":
                correct = correct + 1
            else:
                wrong_amount = wrong_amount + 1
            print("Seed:", seed, "|", review, "|", reply)

    print("Correct replies:", correct, "of", attempts, "attempts")
    print("Assessable replies:", correct + wrong_amount, "of", attempts)
    print("Wrong amount:", wrong_amount, "| Incomplete:", incomplete,
          "| Timeout:", timeouts)
```

**Expected output.** At 0.2, one attempt never gives you a reply, so you can assess three replies. At 0.8, one attempt gives you some text but no amount. You can still assess three replies. Two out of those three gave a correct amount.

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. **Using an existing model.** Inference is what happens when the assistant uses the trained model to produce a reply. Training is the earlier process of adjusting the model using examples. This lesson varies how replies are generated from an existing model.
2. **The next choice depends on what came before.** “The minimum is” can lead to an amount. “The minimum balance” may lead to “is” first. The model works out new chances with the chosen piece included.
3. **It can appear in the weighted draw.** Greedy selection picks “is,” the strongest choice. The 10% choice occupies positions 90 through 99 in the toy draw, so it can win by sampling.
4. **The probabilities stay as they were.** Dividing 1 by 1 gives an exponent of 1 in our sketch. That says nothing about whether the resulting text gives the correct amount. Check the answer against the policy.
5. **The second choice crosses the line.** 60% alone falls short, and adding 30% takes the total to 90%. Keep the whole second choice, then rescale the two kept chances.
6. **The seed is one part of the setup.** Save the actual request, model snapshot if available, and generation settings too. The service and its computations can also change. Check how consistently the system repeats under the conditions you can document.
7. **Read the stop reason and saved text.** A length stop may leave part of the reply. A timeout with no reply leaves no text to judge. A wrong amount is a factual error you can show by comparing a claim against the policy.
8. **Say which attempts each count includes.** Two of four attempts delivered a correct reply. Two of three replies with an amount were correct. One attempt gave you no amount to judge. Report the reason if known, such as a timeout or a reply that cut off.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling every wording change a mistake.** Compare what each answer says. “Five thousand rupees” and “₹5,000” can express the same requirement.
- **Expecting one draw to reveal the effect of a setting.** Repeat both settings. Read the individual answers before interpreting the counts.
- **Assuming lower temperature fixes incorrect answers.** It can give a wrong but favored choice even more weight. Check the policy claims directly.
- **Reading top-p as a fixed number of choices.** Start at the strongest and add their chances. The total you need to reach determines how many you keep.
- **Saving the seed and forgetting the rest of the request.** Save the full input, snapshot if available, other settings, and outcomes. Look for changes when two runs disagree.
- **Dropping short or missing replies from the report.** Record what ended the attempt. Report all attempts as well as the subset you could assess.
