## Beginner Explanation

### Find what changed before repeating

Read the 90%, 70%, and 80% alongside their run logs. First identify what was measured. If a run answered ten questions, 90% can mean nine passed judgments out of ten valid binary results. Check the counts and the criteria before comparing that score with another percentage.

- To investigate assistant variation, ask the same questions again under the same generation procedure. Keep the judge and scoring rule fixed. Save each answer so you can see which wording changed.
- To investigate judge variation, replay the exact same frozen answers against the same rubric and judge configuration. Compare the individual verdicts on each answer.
- To investigate the effect of the questions sampled, freeze the settings and document which case IDs were selected. A set with more deadline questions can score differently even without a change to the assistant or judge.
- To evaluate a configuration change, choose which factor to change and record both versions. A different rubric, judge, or aggregation rule can move the score even when every reply stays fixed.

A **seed** is a value used to initialize a random procedure. Record it, or the planned seed sequence, along with the model version, prompt, sampling settings, and scoring versions. The **effective configuration** is what actually ran, including resolved defaults. Check whether a requested setting was applied. Preserve the dataset and generation procedure or exact response records, judge, rubric, aggregation, and run ID. A seed can help reproduce a sampling procedure without guaranteeing that a live service produces the same result.

If the evaluation uses a service that loads work lazily, record whether each run started cold, before a first request warmed it, or warm, after a documented warm-up. Compare like starts when measuring latency. A timeout can also change which scores enter a quality mean. Record it before trying to explain a change in the mean.

Running the same arithmetic twice is a deterministic repeat. Replaying saved judge outcomes verifies the report and the decisions that follow from those outcomes. Generating fresh live replies or asking a live judge to score again measures another source of variation. The Python in this lesson replays invented data.

### Read the observed scores before summarizing

Suppose two more runs return 80% and 80%. The five scores are `70, 80, 90, 80, 80`. Put them on a number line and place one mark at 70, three at 80, and one at 90. This pattern of observed scores is their **distribution**.

Add the five values to get 400. Divide by the five runs to get 80. This arithmetic **mean** describes the center of the observed scores. It leaves the spread out of view.

Sort the scores to get `70, 80, 80, 80, 90`. The middle score is 80, the **median**. With an even number of scores, average the middle two. If the 90 were instead a 20, the sum would fall to 330 and the mean to 66. The middle score would still be 80. Inspect the 20. It could be a real failure or a run with a configuration change.

The minimum is 70 and the maximum is 90. Their difference is 20 percentage points, the **range**. It shows the distance between the observed extremes. It does not tell how many runs were near either end.

### Measure distance from the mean

Put 70 and 90 on either side of the mean, 80. They are both ten points from the center. If you subtract 80 from each of the scores `70, 80, 90`, you get `−10, 0, 10`. Adding those differences would give zero and hide both ten-point gaps. Square each difference and the two gaps both contribute 100. The middle score contributes zero. The total is 200.

Divide 200 by all three observed runs to get 66.67, the **population variance of these three saved scores**. Its units are squared percentage points because the distances were squared. Take the square root to get **population standard deviation**, about 8.16 percentage points. It summarizes observed spread in the same units as the scores. It gives no guaranteed bound on a future run.

If you wanted to estimate a wider population’s variance from a suitable sample under the usual assumptions, the standard **sample variance** calculation would divide by one less than the number of observations. For these three, it would give 100. This lesson uses population variance throughout to describe the complete lists on screen. Repeated scores from the same case do not automatically satisfy the independence assumptions for inference.

### Choose what counts as one observation

If you read Q1 twice and Q2 twice, you have four recorded observations and two distinct questions. An **analysis unit** is what you count as one observation for a particular statement. Use the case to report question coverage. Use a repeated attempt within that case to describe how its scores changed.

Here is a small **case-by-setting plot**. Each cell uses the same 50–100 scale. Read left to right, with each character advancing two points. An asterisk marks each observed score. The exact scores are printed beside the marks. The numbers are presentation scores. Both settings saw the same case IDs and a fixed rubric.

| Case and slice | Setting X, two observations | Setting Y, two observations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R, routine | `......................*.*.` 94, 98 | `....................*.*...` 90, 94 |
| H, deadline | `....*.*...................` 58, 62 | `...........*.*............` 72, 76 |

Within-case variation is the spread of repeats on the same question and setting. For X on R, the scores differ by four points. For X, the case means are 96 and 60. Their difference shows **between-case variation**, the change in the center from one question to another. Here is the variance version of the same comparison. Within either X case the two distances from its mean are −2 and 2, giving population variance (4 + 4) ÷ 2 = 4. Compare the two case means, 96 and 60, around their own center of 78. Their population variance is (324 + 324) ÷ 2 = 324. Keep the unit next to each result. Repeats on R share whatever makes R easy or hard, so four observations across R and H still give you only two distinct questions.

A **slice** is a group of questions that share a trait. With equal weight on routine and deadline, X scores (96 + 60) ÷ 2 = 78 and Y scores (92 + 74) ÷ 2 = 83. Now give routine 90% of the weight and deadline 10%, using the same weights for both settings. X scores 0.90 × 96 + 0.10 × 60 = 92.4. Y scores 0.90 × 92 + 0.10 × 74 = 90.2. The order reverses even though every saved score stays fixed. Choose and document slice weights before comparing. These two weighting plans answer different questions.

### Choose an aggregation and show its denominator

To summarize one case across runs, average its eligible repeat scores. To summarize one run across cases, average its eligible scores across the cases in that run. An equal-run mean gives every run the same weight, even when one run scored fewer rows. This is a **macro** mean over runs. A pooled **micro** mean gives every valid, scorable row the same weight. Weighting each run mean by its number of scored rows reproduces the micro result. An equal-case macro mean is another option if each distinct case should contribute equally.

A missing result can come from a skipped case or a timeout. If failures are more likely on deadline questions, excluding those rows can make a mean look better by changing the case mix. An invalid result might be unreadable. A valid abstention is readable and says no numeric judgment was made. Set these aside with reasons rather than filling in zero. In the audit report below, **attempted / planned** includes timeouts, **valid / attempted** counts usable verdict records including abstentions, and **scorable / valid** counts numeric verdicts. Also show **scorable / planned** as end-to-end coverage. Keep denominators visible and use “unavailable” when there is nothing to divide by.

A critical policy failure remains a failure even if its presentation score is high or missing. Count it separately and keep its case ID. A consistent presentation score can be useful if predictable wording matters. Stable bad advice still needs to be fixed. A small set of repeats helps find cases to inspect. It cannot establish live reliability or justify a precise confidence claim.

## Worked Demonstration

### Example 1. Replace a headline with the observed spread

**User question.** What should the team conclude after the first 90% refund-policy test?

**Approved reference.** Use the same `refund-1` cases and pass rule for the saved repeats.

**Actual LLM response.** The fictional batch of five runs yields 90%, 70%, 80%, 80%, and 80%.

**Assessment.** The total is 400 and there are five results, so the mean is 80%. Sort them to see that the median is also 80%. The lowest and highest are 70% and 90%, giving a range of 20 percentage points.

**Failure classification.** Treat 90% as one observed result. Review the 70% run by case ID, and compare the effective configuration and scorable counts before attributing the difference to generation or judging.

### Example 2. Compare runs with the same center

**User question.** Which setting gives more predictable presentation scores in these saved runs?

**Approved reference.** The rubric, judge and refund questions are fixed for this toy comparison. Policy is verified separately.

**Actual LLM response.** The saved scores are 78, 80, 82 for the steadier setting and 70, 80, 90 for the wider setting.

**Assessment.** Both means are 80. The first series has distances −2, 0 and 2. The squared distances total 8, so population variance is 8/3 ≈ 2.67 and standard deviation is about 1.63. The second series has squared distances totaling 200. Population variance is 200/3 ≈ 66.67 and standard deviation is about 8.16.

**Failure classification.** The first setting varied less in these runs. Open the 70-point run for the second setting and look for the cases that pulled it down. Then open the policy checks for the steadier setting too. If it repeatedly tells customers they get 90 days, its consistent presentation is no reason to release it.

### Example 3. Count what each average leaves out

**User question.** Has setting X produced enough evidence to release the refund assistant?

**Approved reference.** A request for an eligible item must be made through calendar day 30 after delivery and with a valid receipt. Presentation scores measure how the advice is communicated.

**Actual LLM response.** In X1, Q1 earns 90, Q2 earns 70, and Q3 earns 90 while promising a 90-day deadline. Q4 is skipped. In X2, Q1 earns 100, Q2 returns an invalid verdict, Q3 has a valid abstention, and Q4 times out.

**Assessment.** X1’s mean is 250/3 ≈ 83.33 and X2’s is 100/1 = 100. Equal run weight gives 91.67. Pooling the scored rows gives (250 + 100) ÷ (3 + 1) = 87.50. Report 7/8 attempted, 5/7 valid, 4/5 scorable, and 4/8 scorable end to end. The 90-day reply still fails the policy even though its presentation scored 90.

**Failure classification.** Block release on the verified critical violation. Inspect the invalid verdict and timeout, and repeat only after identifying the failed stage. If the question is how results change with a different case mix, test declared slice weights on fixed cases, as in the case-by-setting plot above. Do not compare the averages without their denominators.

### Step 3 — Measure spread around a fixed mean

```python
print(f"Population SD: {pstdev(scores):.2f}")
```

### Step 5 — Give runs or scored rows equal weight

Start with two presentation scores for each of two runs. The dictionary under a run ID stores case IDs and scores. `set().union(*runs.values())` collects distinct case IDs from all nested dictionaries. `sorted()` gives them a stable output order. The list expression after `values =` collects a case’s score from every run that has one.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from statistics import mean

runs = {"R1": {"Q1": 90, "Q2": 70}, "R2": {"Q1": 100, "Q2": 80}}
case_ids = sorted(set().union(*runs.values()))
for name in case_ids:
    values = [run[name] for run in runs.values() if name in run]
    print(f"{name}: mean {mean(values):.2f} ({len(values)} scored)")

run_means, pooled = [], []
for run_id, run in runs.items():
    run_means.append(mean(run.values()))
    pooled.extend(run.values())

print(f"Equal-run mean: {mean(run_means):.2f}")
print(f"Pooled score mean: {mean(pooled):.2f}")
```

### Step 7 — Reproduce a score report and its release decision

Compare two fictional sampling settings on the same four planned questions and the same presentation rubric. The reply records include wording and a separate policy decision supplied by a human reviewer. A row is a planned run–case pair. The report saves the case, reply ID and text, original and replayed verdict values, effective settings, policy result, and action. A policy failure takes priority over an unscorable verdict. Keep all observed rows and the score filters so another evaluator can reconstruct the report.

`observed` holds the historical fixture. The `changes` dictionary describes independent replay variants. Looking up a replacement with `.get()` leaves the historical rows intact. Check the original raw value beside the replayed value in the audit. For the spread of both per-run means and pooled scores we use population standard deviation, using all the saved values in the corresponding list.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from statistics import mean, median, pvariance, pstdev

policy = "Refund requests are allowed through calendar day 30 after delivery with a valid receipt."
dataset = "refund-eval-1"
rubric = "presentation-v1"
judge = "fictional-judge-r1 | temperature 0"
judge_replay = "saved-verdicts-1"
revision = "baseline"
questions = {
    "Q1": "What is the refund deadline?",
    "Q2": "Do I need a receipt for an eligible refund?",
    "Q3": "Can I still request a refund 45 days after delivery?",
    "Q4": "Can I ask for a refund on day 30 with my receipt?",
}
# Text and independently reviewed policy result.
replies = {
    "deadline": ("Ask through calendar day 30 after delivery.", "PASS"),
    "receipt": ("You need a valid receipt.", "PASS"),
    "wrong": ("You can still request a refund. You have 90 days after delivery with a valid receipt.", "FAIL"),
    "expired": ("Day 45 is outside the 30-calendar-day refund window after delivery.", "PASS"),
    "boundary": ("Yes. Day 30 after delivery is included, with a valid receipt.", "PASS"),
}
generation = {"X": "refund-assistant-r1 | temperature 0.7", "Y": "refund-assistant-r1 | temperature 0.2"}
# Setting, generation seed, start state. These are saved, effective fixture values.
runs = {
    "X1": ("X", 41, "warm"), "X2": ("X", 42, "warm"),
    "Y1": ("Y", 41, "warm"), "Y2": ("Y", 42, "warm"),
}
# Captured reply ID, attempt flag, raw result. An absent row was never attempted.
observed = {
    "X1": {"Q1": ("deadline", True, 90), "Q2": ("receipt", True, 70), "Q3": ("wrong", True, 90)},
    "X2": {"Q1": ("deadline", True, 100), "Q2": ("receipt", True, "bad"), "Q3": ("expired", True, "ABSTAIN"),
           "Q4": (None, True, None)},
    "Y1": {"Q1": ("deadline", True, 88), "Q2": ("receipt", True, 76), "Q3": ("expired", True, 84),
           "Q4": ("boundary", True, 82)},
    "Y2": {"Q1": ("deadline", True, 90), "Q2": ("receipt", True, 78), "Q3": ("expired", True, 80),
           "Q4": ("boundary", True, 82)},
}
changes = {"baseline": {}, "rescore": {("X1", "Q3"): 60},
           "repair": {("X2", "Q2"): 80}, "abstain": {("Y2", "Q4"): "ABSTAIN"}}

def status(attempted, reply, raw):
    if not attempted:
        return "SKIPPED"
    if reply is None:
        return "TIMEOUT"
    if raw == "ABSTAIN":
        return "ABSTAIN"
    if type(raw) is int and 0 <= raw <= 100:
        return "SCORED"
    return "INVALID"

def fraction(count, total):
    return f"{count}/{total}" if total else "unavailable"

audit = []
for run, (setting, seed, start) in runs.items():
    for case, question in questions.items():
        reply, attempted, original = observed[run].get(case, (None, False, None))
        raw = changes[revision].get((run, case), original)
        text, gate = replies.get(reply, (None, "UNKNOWN"))
        validity = status(attempted, reply, raw)
        action = "BLOCK" if gate == "FAIL" else ("REVIEW" if validity != "SCORED" or gate == "UNKNOWN" else "CHECKED")
        audit.append({
            "run": run, "setting": setting, "dataset": dataset, "policy": policy,
            "policy_version": "refund-1", "generation": generation[setting],
            "seed": seed, "start": start, "rubric": rubric, "judge": judge, "judge_replay": judge_replay,
            "case": case, "question": question, "reply": reply, "text": text,
            "attempted": attempted, "original": original, "raw": raw,
            "status": validity, "gate": gate, "review": action, "revision": revision,
            "aggregation": "pooled scorable rows and equal-run mean",
        })

print(f"{dataset} | {rubric} | {judge_replay} | revision {revision}")
for setting, effective in generation.items():
    print(f"Setting {setting}: {effective}")
    rows = [row for row in audit if row["setting"] == setting]
    scores = [row["raw"] for row in rows if row["status"] == "SCORED"]
    run_means = []
    for run in runs:
        if runs[run][0] != setting:
            continue
        values = [row["raw"] for row in rows if row["run"] == run and row["status"] == "SCORED"]
        average = f"{mean(values):.2f}" if values else "unavailable"
        print(f"  {run}: {average} ({len(values)} scored)")
        if values:
            run_means.append(mean(values))

    attempted = sum(row["attempted"] for row in rows)
    valid = sum(row["status"] in ("SCORED", "ABSTAIN") for row in rows)
    print(f"  attempted {fraction(attempted, len(rows))} | valid {fraction(valid, attempted)} | "
          f"scorable {fraction(len(scores), valid)} | end-to-end {fraction(len(scores), len(rows))}")
    if scores:
        print(f"  micro mean {mean(scores):.2f} | median {median(scores):.2f} | "
              f"min {min(scores)} | max {max(scores)} | range {max(scores) - min(scores)}")
        print(f"  row population variance {pvariance(scores):.2f} | row population SD {pstdev(scores):.2f}")
        print(f"  macro run mean {mean(run_means):.2f} | run-mean population SD {pstdev(run_means):.2f}")
    else:
        print("  score summaries unavailable")

    gaps = [f"{row['run']}/{row['case']}:{row['status']}" for row in rows if row["status"] != "SCORED"]
    failures = [f"{row['run']}/{row['case']}" for row in rows if row["gate"] == "FAIL"]
    print("  gaps " + (", ".join(gaps) or "none"))
    print("  critical " + (", ".join(failures) or "none observed"))
    needs_review = any(row["review"] == "REVIEW" for row in rows)
    decision = "BLOCK" if failures else ("REVIEW" if needs_review else "CONTINUE EVALUATION")
    print(f"  decision {decision}")

sample = next(row for row in audit if (row["run"], row["case"]) == ("X1", "Q3"))
print(f"Audit X1/Q3: {sample['reply']} | original {sample['original']} | "
      f"replay {sample['raw']} | policy {sample['gate']} | decision {sample['review']}")
```

## Knowledge Check

1. Two runs disagree on the same saved reply. What would you fix in the experiment to investigate judge variation?
2. What does an 80% mean tell you about a series with scores 70%, 80%, and 90%?

4. Two lists have the same minimum and maximum. What facts about their distribution could still differ?
5. When would you divide by the number of values for variance, and what does the square root give you?
6. One run has twice as many scored cases as another. How does the pooled mean weight the two runs?
7. A judge output is a valid abstention. Which coverage counts include it and which score count excludes it?
8. A setting consistently gives polished but wrong deadline advice. What should the engineering decision use?

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. Keep each captured reply, reference, rubric, effective judge configuration, and scoring rule fixed. Compare repeated judge verdicts by case rather than regenerating the reply.
2. The observed center is 80%. The mean gives no count of distinct cases and no explanation for the low or high runs.
3. Sorting yields 20, 70, 80, 80, 80. The middle position still holds 80. The mean changes because the sum changes.
4. The values inside the extremes could be clustered in the middle or near the edges. Their means, medians, and standard deviations may differ.
5. Divide by the full count to describe the population variance of the saved list. Its square root is population standard deviation, in the same units as the original scores.
6. The run with twice as many scored cases contributes twice as many rows, so it receives twice the weight in the pooled mean. An equal-run mean gives each run half.
7. Count the attempt and valid record. Leave it out of the scorable count and numeric mean. Report the abstention as its own status.
8. Apply the independent policy rule and block the confirmed contradiction. Use the score series to understand presentation stability and collect more paired evidence after correcting the policy behavior.

## Common Mistakes

When percentages move, open the run and case records first. Identify which settings applied, which answers were reused or generated anew, and which judge outputs were valid.

Repeated values on one question reveal what happened on that question. Keep the number of distinct questions separate.

Publish score coverage and the policy gate beside any average. Missing scores change the denominator, and a high presentation score can accompany a wrong policy claim.

Replay saved outcomes to reproduce the math. Use controlled repeats to investigate response or judge variability. Keep the analysis unit, variance convention, weights, and coverage clear. Inspect critical facts and missing evidence before drawing a release conclusion.
