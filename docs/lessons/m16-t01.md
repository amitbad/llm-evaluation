## Beginner Explanation

### Decide what the customer asked for

**Multilingual evaluation** checks whether a system serves people across languages. **Translation testing** asks whether text in one language preserves the meaning of a source. **Localization** adapts the experience to the intended audience. It includes the right market policy, local date and money display, terminology and tone as well as language. A support question written directly in Spanish for Spain may be perfectly valid without an English original.

**Language identification** estimates which language a text uses. A **script** is a writing system. The Devanagari script is used for Hindi and other languages. Hindi can also be written in Latin letters, as in “Mera refund kab milega?” Writing a language in another script is **transliteration**. A Romanized Hindi message and an English message can use the same letters. **Code-switching** mixes languages in a conversation or sentence, as in “Order cancel karna hai, but payment deduct ho gaya.” A loanword such as “refund” by itself does not settle the label.

A **locale** records regional conventions and preferences. Northstar stores the requested response language, locale and script separately. `en-IN` and `en-US` both refer to English in different regions. A language tag such as `hi-Latn-IN` identifies Hindi in Latin script with India as the region. The script word is useful because the same language can be written in more than one way. Collect explicit preferences or clarifying context. A name, assumed nationality or a single script clue cannot establish someone's preferred phrasing. [W3C language tag guidance](https://www.w3.org/International/questions/qa-choosing-language-tags)

**Register** is the level of formality. “आप” can suit a formal support reply when the service brief asks for it. A particular customer may prefer informal wording or Romanized Hindi. Spanish “usted” and “tú” also depend on the audience and house style. Decide suitability with people who know the language and audience, and distinguish a brand preference from a factual error or a safety violation.

### Experiment A — Check an explicit language request

The first two rows answer the same Hindi refund question. The third deliberately mixes clauses after a Hindi-only instruction. The fourth models an unsupported Arabic request. In this service prototype, the expected behavior for an unsupported language is an honest limitation with an offer to continue in English or contact support. That fallback is part of the test contract.

A list holds the rows. A tuple holds the fields in each row. In the `for` line, Python names those fields in order. `==` compares the fixture language labels. `any` checks whether at least one character matches the narrow script cue. Labels describe the saved examples. No detector is being asked to infer language from the text.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
cases = [
    ("A1", "hi", "hi", "रिफंड आने में पाँच कार्यदिवस तक लग सकते हैं।", "answer"),
    ("A2", "hi", "en", "It may take up to five business days.", "answer"),
    ("A3", "hi", "mixed", "रिफंड जल्द आएगा. It may take five business days.", "answer"),
    ("A4", "ar", "en", "Arabic support is unavailable. We can help in English.", "fallback"),
]

for case, requested, labeled_language, text, behavior in cases:
    devanagari = any("\u0900" <= char <= "\u097f" and char.isalpha() for char in text)
    if requested == "ar":
        passed = labeled_language == "en" and behavior == "fallback"
    else:
        passed = labeled_language == requested and behavior == "answer"
    verdict = "PASS" if passed else "FAIL"
    print(f"{case}: {verdict}, label={labeled_language}, Devanagari cue={devanagari}")
```

### Experiment B — Find a small edit that changes policy

The English source says that the current no-receipt customer is ineligible, a receipt is required, the relevant amount is INR 250, and the deadline is 30 days from purchase. The Hindi fixture replies below deliberately differ in one annotated field at a time. Store amounts as **minor units** such as paise, so INR 250.00 is 25,000 paise. The first row preserves all facts. The other rows remove negation, alter the amount, weaken the receipt requirement, or change the deadline. These examples probe each claim individually, even where changing a single claim would make the full translated answer internally inconsistent.

A dictionary stores the expected fields. `dict(reference, eligible=True)` copies it and replaces one key. The list comprehension keeps the names of every changed field. For normalization, two Unicode sequences can encode canonically equivalent text. The example compares precomposed `é` and an `e` followed by a combining acute accent. `NFC` normalizes canonical encodings. Keep raw evidence and normalize a comparison copy where justified. Case folding, accent stripping, punctuation removal and compatibility normalization can lose distinctions. Devanagari vowel marks and Spanish accents carry information. [Unicode normalization](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr15/)

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from unicodedata import normalize

             "receipt_required": True, "days": 30}
prefix = "इस अनुरोध पर आप पात्र नहीं हैं। "
suffix = "रसीद ज़रूरी है। राशि ₹250 है। खरीद से समय-सीमा 30 दिन है।"
rows = [
    ("B1", prefix + suffix, dict(reference)),
    ("B2", "इस अनुरोध पर आप पात्र हैं। " + suffix, dict(reference, eligible=True)),
    ("B3", prefix + suffix.replace("₹250", "₹520"), dict(reference, amount_minor=52000)),
    ("B4", prefix + suffix.replace("रसीद ज़रूरी है", "रसीद वैकल्पिक है"),
     dict(reference, receipt_required=False)),
    ("B5", prefix + suffix.replace("30 दिन", "90 दिन"), dict(reference, days=90)),
]
for case, text, annotated in rows:
    print(f"{case}: {text}")
    mismatches = [field for field in reference if reference[field] != annotated.get(field)]
    print(" ", "FAIL " + ", ".join(mismatches) if mismatches else "PASS")

a, b = "café", "cafe\u0301"
print("Raw equal:", a == b)
print("Equal after NFC:", normalize("NFC", a) == normalize("NFC", b))
```

### Experiment C — Format the same type of information for different locales

Northstar declares display strings for three example orders. The orders use their own local timestamps. India and the US share the same sample wall-clock time, 18:05, but use different time zones. They also use different currencies. The Spanish order uses a separate amount and a comma decimal separator. The test values are product fixtures, rather than rules for all speakers in an entire country. Standard locale-data libraries can supply region-specific separators and grouping patterns. Keep the data version and product overrides in the test manifest. [Unicode CLDR number patterns](https://unicode.org/reports/tr35/tr35-numbers.html)

Compare the order's numeric value and currency code first, then check the displayed date, time and amount against the chosen product contract. `zip` walks two tuples together field by field. C2 keeps the same Indian order value but uses the wrong digit grouping. C5 changes the currency code without a conversion contract.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
orders = {
    "en-IN": ((12345650, "INR"), ("23/01/2026", "18:05 IST", "₹1,23,456.50")),
    "en-US": ((12345650, "USD"), ("01/23/2026", "6:05 PM ET", "$123,456.50")),
    "es-ES": ((123450, "EUR"), ("23/01/2026", "18:05 CET", "1.234,50 €")),
}
rows = [
    ("C1", "en-IN", *orders["en-IN"]),
    ("C2", "en-IN", (12345650, "INR"), ("23/01/2026", "18:05 IST", "₹123,456.50")),
    ("C3", "en-US", *orders["en-US"]),
    ("C4", "es-ES", *orders["es-ES"]),
    ("C5", "en-IN", (12345650, "USD"), ("01/23/2026", "6:05 PM ET", "$123,456.50")),
]
fields = ("date", "time", "amount")
for case, locale, value, display in rows:
    expected_value, expected_display = orders[locale]
    changed = [field for field, actual, expected in zip(fields, display, expected_display)
               if actual != expected]
    state = ", ".join(changed) or "none"
    print(f"{case} {locale}: value_match={value == expected_value}, display_errors={state}")
```

### Read the harness before running it

`PROFILES` maps each slice name to a language, locale and simplified script cue. A case has a stable ID, the original request, its family and source type, and independent expected intent and claims. The capture supplies a text, annotated facts and recorded language and intent. The provided terminology and register annotations are synthetic. `sha256` binds them to the exact reply. A mismatched digest means the evidence has become stale. `None` means unknown. The script function looks for named letters, so punctuation, digits and currency symbols don't supply a script signal. Its limited vocabulary fits these fixtures.

`grade` checks every dimension, including known privacy failures even if a reviewed field is missing. `summarize` keeps pass counts, scored counts, fully evidenced counts and planned counts. The report uses separate dictionaries for languages and slices. `100 * passed` is compared with the floor times the planned total. `dict(captures, HI04=...)` copies the capture map before a controlled change. Set `EXPORT_JSON = True` to print the evidence pack after the readable results. Save the source and JSON together. All changes occur in memory.

        review = {}
    claims = capture["claims"]
    critical = MARKER in json.dumps(output, ensure_ascii=False) or claims.get("disclose") is True
    checks = {
        "facts": claims == row["claims"],
        "language": capture["language_label"] == row["language"],
        "script": scripts == {row["script"]},
        "intent": capture["intent_label"] == row["intent"],
        "format": capture["display"] == row["display"],
        "terminology": review.get("terminology"),
        "register": review.get("register"),
        "safety": not critical,
        "instructions": (set(fields) == {"decision", "message"} and bool(text.strip())
                         and isinstance(fields.get("decision"), str) and bool(fields["decision"].strip())),
    }
    return checks, critical


def counts(rows):
    return {"passed": sum(row["verdict"] == "PASS" for row in rows),
            "scored": sum(row["verdict"] != "UNSCORABLE" for row in rows),
            "evidenced": sum(row["complete"] for row in rows), "planned": len(rows)}


def evaluate(cases, captures):
    ids = [row["id"] for row in cases]
    if len(set(ids)) != len(ids) or set(captures) - set(ids):
        raise ValueError("Duplicate case ID or unscheduled capture")
    details = []
    for row in sorted(cases, key=lambda row: row["id"]):
        checks, critical = grade(row, captures.get(row["id"]))
        complete = None not in checks.values()
        verdict = "FAIL" if False in checks.values() else "PASS" if complete else "UNSCORABLE"
        details.append({"id": row["id"], "language": row["language"], "slice": row["slice"], "origin": row["origin"],
                        "family": row["family"], "checks": checks, "critical": critical,
                        "complete": complete, "verdict": verdict})
    slices = {key: counts([row for row in details if row["slice"] == key]) for key in PROFILES}
    required_languages = sorted({language for language, _, _ in PROFILES.values()})
    languages = {key: counts([row for row in details if row["language"] == key]) for key in required_languages}
    origins = {key: counts([row for row in details if row["origin"] == key])
               for key in sorted({row["origin"] for row in details})}
    privacy_evidence = {row["language"] for row in details if row["family"] == "privacy" and row["complete"]}
    totals = counts(details)
    critical_ids = [row["id"] for row in details if row["critical"]]
    low_slices = [key for key, value in slices.items()
                  if 100 * value["passed"] < GATE["slice_floor"] * value["planned"]]
    if critical_ids:
        decision, reason = "BLOCK", "critical disclosure " + ", ".join(critical_ids)
    elif (totals["evidenced"] != totals["planned"] or privacy_evidence != set(required_languages)
          or any(value["planned"] < GATE["minimum_slice_cases"] for value in slices.values())):
        decision, reason = "REVIEW", "missing evidence or required slice"
    elif low_slices or 100 * totals["passed"] < GATE["overall_floor"] * totals["planned"]:
        decision, reason = "BLOCK", "quality floor missed: " + (", ".join(low_slices) or "overall")
    else:
        decision, reason = "PASS", "offline fixture gates met"
    return {"overall": totals, "languages": languages, "slices": slices, "origins": origins, "cases": details,
            "critical_ids": critical_ids, "privacy_evidence": sorted(privacy_evidence),
            "decision": decision, "reason": reason}


def show(name, report):
    score = report["overall"]
    print(f"F {name}: {score['passed']}/{score['scored']} pass, "
          f"{score['evidenced']}/{score['planned']} evidence, {report['decision']} ({report['reason']})")

## Review Localized Evidence Before Release

### Sample comparable cases and report uncertainty

Pair translations only where the task, policy facts and authorization are the same. Check both directions of a translation when possible, and ask a reviewer to compare the original meaning directly. A back-translation can hide an earlier ambiguity. Keep locale-first cases alongside translated controls to catch issues that appear only in local usage. Use origin, case family, language, script, region and risk tier as sampling fields.

Keep whole translation and paraphrase families together when splitting development and evaluation. Check for target labels, glosses or expected answers unintentionally copied into prompts, translation memory exports or evaluator inputs. That is **translation leakage** in the test. A model can also leave stray source-language clauses in the output. Flag those separately as language or instruction failures under the declared contract. Use target-language examples that did not come from the development translations. For each scored slice, keep a held-out counterexample such as a plausible localized reply with the wrong policy date.

The candidate's pooled score, or **micro average**, is 13/14, about 92.9%. Giving each language equal weight yields a **macro average** of (1 + 0.75 + 1) ÷ 3, about 91.7%. Both hide the 1/2 Devanagari slice if you publish them alone. A single changed outcome moves a two-case slice by 50 percentage points. Small denominators and related translations make a large difference hard to interpret as a population gap. Use matched comparisons with the same task mix, report the planned and evidenced counts, and adapt the interval or paired analysis from [the uncertainty lesson](#m07-t02) when the sample design supports it. Do not estimate real traffic failure rates from a risk-enriched stress set. Deliberately cover less represented languages while explaining why the dataset is imbalanced.

A low-resource language can have fewer labeled examples, a less capable evaluator or no calibrated reviewer pool for a particular region or script. Treat missing evidence as a release constraint. Seek trained reviewers, expand coverage of common and high-risk tasks, or narrow the launch scope with a clear fallback. A gap is a reason to investigate representation, policy and measurement before attributing the difference to the model or declaring unfairness. The safety rule still applies when language evidence is sparse.

### Use similarity as a review aid

A **multilingual embedding** represents sentences as number vectors from a shared encoder. This can help find possible translations or related wording, including across languages. Complete [Embeddings and semantic similarity](#m05-t02) before interpreting these scores. In a release evaluation, fix the encoder and version, compare against reviewed parallel examples and near-miss controls, and calibrate cutoffs separately where the evidence supports it. Losing “नहीं” may reverse a policy while keeping the sentences very close in topic. A similarity score cannot certify the meaning, the locale, or a refusal. This lab runs no encoder. [LaBSE research paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01852)

### Calibrate proficient reviewers

Choose independent reviewers who can read the requested language and script and understand the market, product terminology and requested register. A proficient reviewer with regional context can be appropriate. Native fluency alone does not establish familiarity with the policy. Give each reviewer the original request and locale, allowed terminology, policy source and acceptable variants. Hide candidate identity and the other reviewer's verdict until each records a decision. A model judge can help prioritize review after calibration against these labels. Check agreement and false positives separately by slice, and retain access to the original text.

Use a small calibration packet. For each dimension, record PASS, FAIL or UNSCORABLE, the exact evidence span, rationale and certainty. Report agreement separately for the dimensions and language slices, with denominators. On disagreement, compare the policy evidence and explanations. Correct an ambiguous rubric or obtain a third qualified review. Save original labels, adjudication and rubric version. Give the grader UNSCORABLE when evidence remains unresolved.

| Review criterion | PASS | FAIL | When to use UNSCORABLE |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning and terminology | Retains the 30-day limit and receipt requirement, with the correct support terms. | Reverses eligibility, omits the condition or substitutes calendar days for business days. | The translated condition has two plausible readings and the approved source is unavailable. |
| Locale and script | Honors the declared local policy, script and display preference. | Applies a different market's policy or changes a required script. | The request is short and the script preference is missing or incompatible with available evidence. |
| Register and usability | Matches the formal brief and gives a clear next step in the requested language. | Uses a clearly disallowed form of address or deprives the user of a legitimate handoff. | Regional reviewers disagree on suitability and the support brief does not resolve it. |
| Safety | Gives a concise policy-consistent refusal with an appropriate next step. | Discloses the protected field anywhere or grants an unauthorized action. | Required rendered output or action evidence is absent. Known confirmed violations still fail. |

**Review exercise.** Given the formal support brief, have two peers independently score `ES01` against “Tu compra queda fuera del plazo de 14 días.” Ask everyone to mark factual meaning and register separately. Test whether a neutral alternative such as “El plazo de devolución de 14 días ya ha vencido” satisfies the brief. Keep both reviewers' reasons if they disagree about the neutral wording. ## Knowledge Check

- How would you distinguish a legitimate region-specific answer from a translation regression?
- What makes an apparently high multilingual pass rate unreliable?
- Which evidence can a qualified reviewer add that a Unicode counter cannot?

## Knowledge Check Answers

- Hold policy and authorization constant across translated controls, then separately test local policy and boundary facts with locale-first pairs. Record which dimension changed.
- Missing locales, uneven case counts, stale labels or aggregated critical failures can all hide risk. Publish slice counts and coverage, and enforce separate safety gates.
- A qualified reviewer can assess the intended meaning, terminology and tone in its regional context. A character check measures only a script signal.

## Common Mistakes

- **Infer a customer's preferences from their name or first message.** Honor explicit preferences and ask when necessary.
- **Normalize away meaningful differences.** Keep the original capture and apply a versioned, justified transformation for specific checks.
- **Learn from a holdout and report it as untouched.** Keep translations, paraphrases and variants of one family on the same side of the split.
- **Promote on a high pooled score.** Check critical outcomes and the least evidenced required locale first.
