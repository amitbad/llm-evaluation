## What You Will Learn

- Turn a broad instruction to score a reply into a rubric with a clear criterion.
- Apply a binary policy check and a low, middle, or high completeness rating to the same reply.
- Review full saved replies without seeing the system that produced them, and record each judgment with its response ID and evidence.
- Distinguish N/A, missing evidence, and a failed answer.
- Use a disputed reply to revise an anchor, then compare the original and revised judgments.

## Why This Lesson Matters

Imagine you’re using Harbor Leaf Bank’s chat assistant to plan next month’s expenses. The reply gives you a fee of ₹100. You still don’t know whether you’ll pay it once or every month. At the bank, one reviewer gives the reply a high score for the right amount. Another gives it a low score for leaving out the timing. Their scores point to different things the bank could do next.

The customer asked what fee applies below the Standard Savings minimum on July 15, 2026. The full reply reads, “A ₹100 fee applies if your balance falls below ₹5,000.” The relevant policy, SV-2 section 2, says a ₹100 monthly fee applies below ₹5,000. No below-minimum fee applies at exactly ₹5,000.

Both reviewers can point to something real in the reply. In this chapter you’ll turn their observations into judgments the bank can reproduce and act on. The bank, policy, saved replies, and sample judgments are fictional. Allow about 4.5 hours for reading, practice, and the assignment.

## Beginner Explanation

### 1. Ask one question at a time

Read the saved reply in the opening. It states the right fee. It never says whether the customer pays once, each week, or each month. Write those two observations on separate lines.

A **criterion** is one question you ask about the reply. A **rubric** is a guide that states those questions and tells reviewers how to give each result. Here are the two questions the bank will use.

- **Policy accuracy.** Does the reply give the correct monthly fee amount for the situation, with every fee claim it makes consistent with the July policy? The amount is required. An omitted frequency is handled by the completeness check, while an explicit wrong frequency fails the accuracy check. Record PASS or FAIL. A judgment with two possible outcomes is a **binary decision** when the criterion applies and the evidence is available.
- **Completeness of fee details.** Does the reply state the fee frequency and the condition that triggers the fee? Count which details appear. Check their accuracy separately. A reply can include both details and still give the wrong frequency.

Use the accuracy result to decide whether a claim needs correction. Use the completeness result to decide whether a customer still needs an explanation.

### 2. Describe what each score looks like

Make three places to put the replies based on what the reply explicitly includes. The fee amount is already covered by the first criterion. For this completeness scale, look for the frequency and the balance condition.

- **Low.** The reply states neither detail. “The fee is ₹100” is a low example.
- **Middle.** It states exactly one. “A monthly fee of ₹100 applies” gives the timing but leaves out the condition.
- **High.** It states both. “The fee is ₹100 per month below ₹5,000” gives the timing and condition.

Each description and its example give a reviewer an **anchor**, something concrete to match a new reply against. The labels form an **ordinal scale**, which means there’s an order from low to high. Treat them as ordered categories. Two middle ratings don’t add up to a high rating.

Try two replies that both start “A ₹100 monthly fee applies.” End the first with “below the minimum” and the second with “under five thousand rupees.” The bank wants the cutoff stated in the answer. The first earns middle because it gives the timing without naming the cutoff. The second earns high because it gives the timing, the ₹5,000 cutoff, and the direction relative to it. Put both examples in the rubric so a reviewer knows how to handle an amount written in words.

### 3. Give reviewers the same evidence

Keep the question, applicable policy, and full reply together. Use a neutral **response ID**, such as R01, to match a judgment back to the exact reply. Hide the system or model labels until the reviewers have scored the answers independently. This is **blind review** for model identity. Reviewers still need the question and policy. Hiding those would make their job harder in a different way.

For each judgment, write an **evidence note**. Quote the words that support the rating and name any required detail that’s missing. To count an omission, read the entire reply. If you only saw a preview, ask for the full text before judging.

Compare the two reviewers’ sheets by response ID. If they disagree, read the notes and revisit the policy and the relevant criterion. Record the original scores and any later decision in separate places. If the rubric changes, give the changed version a name and apply it to all comparable replies before comparing those results.

### 4. Record why a score is unavailable

A customer asks, “What is the daily ATM withdrawal limit?” The full saved reply R04 is “I can’t confirm the daily ATM withdrawal limit from this savings policy.” Neither fee criterion applies to this question, so record **N/A**, or not applicable, for both. If you want to know whether the assistant handles the ATM question well, define a criterion for that task.

If the question is about fees and the team hasn’t found the applicable policy, mark fee accuracy **NEEDS REVIEW**. The criterion applies and the reviewer needs more evidence. If there’s no reply because the request timed out, record **UNSCORABLE** and track the execution failure. Keep each outcome visible and out of the count of decided accuracy judgments.

### Step 1 — Retrieve the whole reply by ID

Start by finding the exact reply you’re about to review. Both replies answer the July fee question. The braces hold a **dictionary**, a set of names and values. Python uses a response ID as the name and the whole reply as its value. ### Step 2 — Apply a binary decision to reviewed facts

Use R02’s full reply above. In this activity, a reviewer has entered two yes-or-no answers, one about the amount and one about the other claims. `True` means yes and `False` means no. `and` requires both answers to be yes. The colon and indented line show what Python runs when the `if` condition holds. `else` handles the other outcome.

```python
amount_matches = True

if amount_matches and other_claims_match:

else:

```


The amount is right, but “every week” contradicts the policy. One failed condition sends the decision to FAIL.

**Experiment.** Imagine a corrected reply that changes only “every week” to “every month.” Change the corresponding reviewer input, `other_claims_match`, to `True` and run again.

Both conditions now hold. The program uses your judgment of the corrected reply. In a real review, save the corrected reply separately with its own ID and evidence note.

```text

```

Both details are now present, so the first branch supplies the rating. Completeness alone asks whether the details are stated. Step 2’s accuracy judgment still has to check that they are right.

**Checkpoint.** Would adding “weekly” change this completeness result? The frequency would still be present. The accuracy result would become FAIL.

### Step 4 — Decide whether a criterion can be scored

Start with R04, the ATM reply. In its row, the three values after the ID mean “a reply arrived,” “this is a fee question,” and “the applicable fee policy is available,” in that order. A reply arrived, so the first value is true. It’s an ATM question, so the second is false and the program will record N/A without needing the third. Square brackets hold the list of rows, and each set of parentheses groups the values for one response. The loop gives each value a name so you can read `if not fee_question` as “if this isn’t a fee question.”

Copy the block and click **Run**.

```python
cases = [
    ("R04", True, False, False),
    ("R05", True, True, False),
    ("R06", False, True, True),
]

for response_id, reply_arrived, fee_question, policy_available in cases:
    if not reply_arrived:
        outcome = "UNSCORABLE"
    elif not fee_question:
        outcome = "N/A"
    elif not policy_available:
        outcome = "NEEDS REVIEW"
    else:
        outcome = "READY TO SCORE"
    print(response_id, outcome)
```

```text
R04 N/A
R05 NEEDS REVIEW
R06 UNSCORABLE
```

A reviewer marked R04 as an ATM reply. R05 answers a fee question but the applicable policy was unavailable. R06 is the attempt where no reply arrived.

**Experiment.** In the R05 entry, change only the last value, `policy_available`, from `False` to `True`. Run again.

```text

R05 READY TO SCORE
R06 UNSCORABLE
```

R05 continues past the evidence check because the reviewer has found the policy. The other outcomes stay the same.

**Checkpoint.** Should you change R05 to PASS now? The evidence is available. A reviewer still has to use it to judge the reply.

### Step 6 — Pair scores by response ID

Two people return their review sheets in different orders. Looking up the ID in each dictionary gives you the scores for the same reply. `for response_id in reviewer_a:` visits the keys in the first dictionary. `==` compares the two selected labels.

Copy the block and click **Run**.

```python
reviewer_a = {"R01": "MIDDLE", "R03": "MIDDLE"}
reviewer_b = {"R03": "HIGH", "R01": "MIDDLE"}

for response_id in reviewer_a:
    first = reviewer_a[response_id]
    second = reviewer_b[response_id]
    if first == second:
        outcome = "AGREE"
    else:
        outcome = "DISAGREE"
    print(response_id, first, second, outcome)
```

```text
R01 MIDDLE MIDDLE AGREE
R03 MIDDLE HIGH DISAGREE
```

The scores for R01 match even though R01 appears in different positions. The disagreement is about R03.

**Experiment.** In a practice copy, imagine reviewer A rereads R03 with the clarified anchor. Change only reviewer A’s R03 value to `"HIGH"` and run again.

```text

```

R03 now compares equal. The reply text and reviewer B’s score stayed the same. In an actual review, keep the two original sheets and record the reviewed score separately.

**Checkpoint.** If the two reviewers agree, has the score been proved correct? Both could misunderstand the same rule. Read their evidence and use the approved policy and rubric to check the decision.

### Step 7 — Summarize two independent review sheets

Count agreement separately for each criterion on the same full replies. Each dictionary entry contains a pair of human judgments. `accuracy_a, detail_a = reviewer_a[response_id]` gives those two labels their own names. A `continue` line moves straight to the next response. Use it when both reviewers say a reply is outside the fee task. If only one reviewer says N/A, put the reply aside and resolve applicability before comparing its ratings.

The program adds one to a **counter**, a number that tracks how many times an event happened. Only rows scored by both reviewers enter these agreement counts. This example assumes both sheets have the same IDs and all other rows have decided scores.

Copy the block and click **Run**.

```python
reviewer_a = {
    "R01": ("PASS", "MIDDLE"),
    "R02": ("FAIL", "HIGH"),
    "R03": ("PASS", "MIDDLE"),
    "R04": ("N/A", "N/A"),
}
reviewer_b = {
    "R04": ("N/A", "N/A"),
    "R03": ("PASS", "HIGH"),
    "R02": ("FAIL", "HIGH"),
    "R01": ("PASS", "MIDDLE"),
}
paired = 0
accuracy_agrees = 0
completeness_agrees = 0
excluded = 0

for response_id in reviewer_a:
    accuracy_a, detail_a = reviewer_a[response_id]
    accuracy_b, detail_b = reviewer_b[response_id]
    if accuracy_a == "N/A" and accuracy_b == "N/A":
        excluded = excluded + 1
        print(response_id, "N/A")
        continue
    if accuracy_a == "N/A" or accuracy_b == "N/A":
        excluded = excluded + 1
        print(response_id, "REVIEW APPLICABILITY")
        continue
    paired = paired + 1
    if accuracy_a == accuracy_b:
        accuracy_agrees = accuracy_agrees + 1
    else:
        print(response_id, "accuracy:", accuracy_a, "vs", accuracy_b)
    if detail_a == detail_b:
        completeness_agrees = completeness_agrees + 1
    else:
        print(response_id, "completeness:", detail_a, "vs", detail_b)

print("Accuracy agreement:", accuracy_agrees, "of", paired)
print("Completeness agreement:", completeness_agrees, "of", paired)
print("Excluded from paired scores:", excluded)
```

```text
R03 completeness: MIDDLE vs HIGH
R04 N/A
Accuracy agreement: 3 of 3
Completeness agreement: 2 of 3
Excluded from paired scores: 1
```

The reviewers agree on accuracy for all three comparable replies, including the one that failed. They agree on completeness for two. R04 is an ATM question, so it stays visible without entering either total.

**Experiment.** Simulate reviewer B accidentally applying the fee rubric to R04. In reviewer B’s dictionary, change only R04’s pair from `("N/A", "N/A")` to `("FAIL", "LOW")`. Run again.

```text
R03 completeness: MIDDLE vs HIGH
R04 REVIEW APPLICABILITY
Accuracy agreement: 3 of 3
Completeness agreement: 2 of 3
Excluded from paired scores: 1
```

R04 now takes the second condition. Keep the same three comparable replies and ask the reviewers to resolve whether the fee criterion applies to R04.

**Checkpoint.** Did the disagreement on R04 make accuracy agreement drop? It is a disagreement about which question to score. Report it separately so the unchanged count can’t hide it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Scoring a general impression.** Ask which criterion applies and quote the relevant words in the reply.
- **Promoting completeness to an accuracy verdict.** A reply can state a wrong frequency clearly and still include all the details.
- **Scoring a preview or an unlabeled reply.** Keep the full reply and response ID together.
- **Counting N/A as a failure.** Report why it was excluded and keep missing evidence and execution failures separate.
- **Erasing a disagreement after reviewing it.** Save the original scores and the reason for the final decision.
- **Calling matching scores proof of correctness.** Check that reviewers used the applicable policy and rubric.

## Lesson Summary and Next Step

Review the whole reply with one criterion at a time. Use concrete anchors so reviewers know which details to look for, hide model names until scores are recorded, and attach a clear evidence note to each response ID. Keep the original judgments when you resolve a disagreement.
