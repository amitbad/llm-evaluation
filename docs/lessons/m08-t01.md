## Why This Lesson Matters

A customer bought something from Northstar Support 45 days ago and asks, “Can I still get a refund?” Imagine you can send the assistant just one of the three notes below.

- **Refunds.** Refund requests require a valid receipt and must be filed within 30 days of purchase.
- **Shipping.** After 45 days without a receipt, contact us for shipping delays.
- **Warranty.** Start a warranty claim from your order.

Choose the note you would send. The shipping note repeats the customer’s number, but the refund note is the one that sets the deadline. A search that rewards shared words can make the opposite choice.

Imagine an assistant sees only the shipping passage. A reply about refunds may still sound plausible, but the log would show that the necessary evidence was missing before the assistant wrote anything. In this lesson you’ll inspect that log, reproduce why the shipping page wins, and test a change against other questions so you can see what it fixes.

## Beginner Explanation

### Get the policy into the request

An assistant has patterns learned during training, stored as numbers called **parameters**. Northstar can also put a policy directly in the **prompt**, the information supplied for the current request. Copying an entire handbook into every prompt wastes room and makes updates hard to track. Looking up a few passages for this question is **retrieval**. Giving the model those passages before it writes is **retrieval-augmented generation**, or **RAG**. If the search misses a rule or the model ignores it, the answer can still be wrong.

Follow the 45-day request through the system.

1. **Ingest and parse.** Take in the refund page, read its fields, and separate its text from its title and version. Keep the original wording.
2. **Clean and chunk.** Make a consistent search copy. Divide the page into passages small enough to inspect or send to the assistant.
3. **Index.** Make a lookup from a term such as `refund` to the IDs of passages containing it.
4. **Process the query.** Use the same rules to turn the customer’s question into searchable terms.
5. **Retrieve, rank, and select.** Find eligible candidate passages, give each a score, and take the first few. These become the selected **context**, the evidence the writer gets.
6. **Generate and evaluate.** The assistant writes an answer. Inspect the passages it received first, then compare its claims with the approved policy.

### Keep track of where a sentence came from

The current refund document has ID `RF`, title “Refund eligibility,” source `kb/refunds`, and version 2. The title helps a person find it, the source identifies the policy page, and the version identifies the revision. Text, publication status, and the access rule travel with that record. Check required fields and duplicate IDs at ingestion. If two different records use `RF`, a replay could point at the wrong policy.

For search, `REFUND, 45 days?` can become `refund`, `45`, `days`. Lowercase both the text and the query. Turn tabs and runs of spaces into single spaces. Ignore sentence punctuation when finding words, while keeping `30` and `45` distinct. Making those consistent changes is **normalization**, and turning the text into searchable pieces is **tokenization**. A model can use a different tokenizer. Removing all digits would erase the policy distinction. Splitting `3.5` into `3` and `5` or merging `2025-03-01` into `20250301` can also change which texts match. Save the original text for review.

If the exact same passage enters through two copies of one policy source and version, it can occupy two result slots. Keep one searchable copy and preserve its provenance. Different sources, versions, permissions, or subtle wording can change the answer, so do not merge passages merely because they look similar. If you deduplicate, keep a trace of any source or evidence IDs you collapse in real evaluations.

### Give the search a useful passage

Suppose one sentence says, “Damaged product refund claims have a separate condition.” The next says, “Report the damage claim within seven days of delivery and attach photos.” A one-sentence passage can point the search toward the damaged-product rule while leaving the deadline and photos behind. A two-sentence passage can carry both.

Put sentences 1 and 2 on one card and sentence 3 on another. A search for “damaged product refund” may return just the first card. The customer would hear that a separate condition exists without learning the deadline or what to send. Copy sentence 2 onto the second card and it can now travel with the seven-day instruction.

Each card represents a **chunk**. The place where a card starts or ends is its **boundary**. **Chunk size** is how much text a card can hold. **Overlap** is the text printed on neighboring cards. With size 2 and overlap 1, the two cards hold sentences 1–2 and 2–3. If both are selected, the assistant sees sentence 2 twice. Larger chunks keep rules together but take up more of the model’s input space, called its **context budget**. Smaller chunks can be precise but lose conditions. A production splitter may count words or model tokens instead of sentences. For example, six tokens at a time with two repeated tokens means starting each new window four tokens later. Test boundaries against real policies and keep each chunk’s ID, source document ID and version.

### Find candidates, then put them in order

If a catalog says `refund → RF-v2:c01, RF-v2:c02` and `receipt → RF-v2:c01`, a query containing both words can look up two lists and combine them. This term-to-passage catalog is an **inverted index**. A set stores each matching ID once. The index points to passages and the separate document records still carry the source wording.

Start by awarding one point per distinct shared term. A ranking score orders the candidates. `top-k` means the first `k` positions, for example three passages when `k = 3`. Set an explicit rule for equal scores, such as comparing chunk IDs, so a rerun produces the same order. A lexical search compares words, so `proof of purchase` may miss a source that says only `receipt`. This gap is a **lexical mismatch**. The opposite also happens. A shared phrase such as `45 days` can point to the wrong subject.

A refinement weights terms by how common they are in the collection. Suppose `refund` appears in one of two chunks and `days` appears in both. Give `days` weight 1. Give `refund` weight `1 + ln(3/2) ≈ 1.405`, where `ln` is the natural logarithm, a function that grows slowly as its input rises. Both matches together score about 2.405. The rule we’ll use is `1 + ln((N + 1)/(df + 1))`, where `N` is the number of eligible chunks and `df` is the number containing the term. The added ones keep the ratio defined at zero counts. Each matching term counts once per chunk. This is a simplified **term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF–IDF) style** weight with binary term presence. Adding a document can change scores for existing documents because the collection counts change.

A term index is **sparse retrieval** because most vocabulary terms are absent from any one passage. **Dense retrieval** compares learned number vectors, called embeddings, that can help recognize related phrasing. **Hybrid retrieval** combines lexical and dense signals, for example by merging two ranked lists using a documented rule. Freeze the collection, permissions, queries, labels, and cutoff when comparing a ranking method. If you change the dense encoder, record its model version and rebuild its index with that encoder. Similarity still needs a relevance check. The lesson’s executable examples use lexical scoring only.

### Decide what evidence counts

Write down the policy evidence each test question needs before running the search. These reviewed answers are **gold relevance judgments**. Use stable source IDs and sentence IDs to say which wording supports a claim. A chunk can contain one needed sentence and still leave out another. When you change chunking, map gold sentence IDs to the new chunk IDs. Compare sentence coverage across both versions because the number of relevant chunks can change.

Take a query with two relevant chunks. If the top three are `[shipping, refund, receipt]`, there is a hit in the first three, two of the three slots are relevant, and both of the two relevant chunks were found. That gives Hit@3 = 1, Precision@3 = 2/3, and Recall@3 = 2/2. If the first relevant chunk is second, its **reciprocal rank** is `1/2`. The **mean reciprocal rank (MRR)** is the average of those fractions across scored queries. A query with known relevant evidence that retrieves none gets reciprocal rank 0. This lesson computes MRR from the full returned ranking before the top-k cut. If you use only the first k results, label the metric MRR@k. Precision here always divides by k, including any empty slots.

One chunk can answer the policy question directly while another can help only with one detail. Give those grades 2 and 1. A reviewed irrelevant passage gets 0. If the complete policy appears first, the writer sees it sooner than if a partial answer appears first. **Normalized discounted cumulative gain (nDCG)** rewards that ordering. At rank 1, grade 2 earns `(2² − 1)/log₂(2) = 3/1 = 3`. At rank 2, grade 1 earns `(2¹ − 1)/log₂(3) ≈ 0.631`. Here `log₂` is a logarithm with base 2. As the rank number increases, so does the divisor, which reduces the credit. For this lesson each contribution is `(2^grade − 1)/log₂(rank + 1)`. Sum the contributions through k to get **DCG**. Divide by the DCG of the ideal order of the known grades at the same cutoff to get nDCG. A value of 1 means the list earned as much credit as that ideal order.

An **unlabeled** passage has yet to be judged. Queue it for review and disclose judgment coverage. For example, if two of the top three are judged, record `2/3 judged`. Treat a calculation that temporarily gives the unknown passage zero credit as provisional. A query with no positive gold judgments cannot support ordinary recall or normalized gain. If a reviewer confirms the corpus lacks an answer, test that separately. If the query is simply unjudged, label that gap and keep it out of the scored mean. Always report the number scored.

### Diagnose before changing the system

Open the saved query, eligible source versions, chunk boundaries, ranked list, and context actually sent to the writer. If the needed refund policy is absent from that context, start with retrieval or selection. If the full policy is present and the answer says “45 days is fine,” investigate generation, the step that wrote the answer. Record both if both are supported by the trace.

- **Wrong subject at the top.** Compare matched terms and weights for that passage and the gold passage.
- **Needed evidence absent from the full ranking.** Check that the current source was ingested, indexed, authorized, and reachable with the query’s wording.
- **Needed evidence ranked below the cutoff.** Try a ranking change or a larger top-k on the same queries. Measure the extra noise and context.
- **Condition separated from the rule.** Read neighboring chunks. Try overlap or a policy-section boundary.
- **Repeated passages crowd the list.** Compare the original source and version, and inspect deduplication before rebuilding.
- **Stale wording appears.** Check the approved version and index refresh. Remove stale versions from eligible search.
- **Restricted material is visible.** Treat as a critical failure. Filter by verified access before ranking, context selection, or display.

A passage can be useful and still be unauthorized. The public replay in this chapter builds the index for the allowed role and approved current versions, so restricted text never appears in any public result. Production permissions must come from a trusted identity and access system. For a real change, also check search latency, freshness after policy updates, and the total input space used by the chosen passages. Freeze the query set and judgments while varying one setting. Use new reviewed queries before generalizing.

## Worked Demonstration

### Example 1. Preserve the condition while changing chunk boundaries

Read the fictional current policy `RF` v2, from `kb/refunds`. It contains these three sentences.

1. “Refund requests require a valid receipt and must be filed within 30 days of purchase.”
2. “Damaged product refund claims have a separate condition.”
3. “Report the damage claim within seven days of delivery and attach photos.”

**User question.** “What do I send for a damaged product refund, and when?”

**Approved reference.** Sentences 2 and 3 establish the subject, seven-day deadline measured from delivery, and photo requirement.

With size 2 and overlap 0, `RF-v2:c01` covers sentences 1–2 and `RF-v2:c02` covers sentence 3. Both keep source `kb/refunds`, version 2, and document ID `RF`. Predict which chunk contains the product type and the photo requirement together. There is no such chunk. With overlap 1, `RF-v2:c01` still covers 1–2 and `RF-v2:c02` now covers 2–3. The chunk IDs are local to a build, so log its settings alongside them. The stable sentence IDs `RF-v2:s02` and `RF-v2:s03` make the evidence comparable.

**Actual LLM response.** Fictional replay fixture, “Check with support for the required documents,” after only the original `c01` was selected.

**Assessment.** The selected text refers to a separate condition but never states it. The overlap supplies a passage with both sentences for a later ranking test.

**Failure classification.** Required evidence split at the chunk boundary. Check whether the new chunk is selected, and measure how much repeated text overlap sends to the writer.

### Example 2. Find why a shipping passage beats the refund policy

**User question.** “Can I get a refund after 45 days without a receipt?”

**Approved reference.** `RF-v2:s01` requires a valid receipt and filing within 30 days of purchase.

Score one point per distinct shared term, using these three fictional single-sentence passages.

- **SH.** “After 45 days without a receipt, contact us for shipping delays.” The shared terms are `after`, `45`, `days`, `without`, `a`, and `receipt`. Score 6.
- **RF.** “Refund requests require a valid receipt and must be filed within 30 days of purchase.” The shared terms are `refund`, `a`, `receipt`, and `days`. Score 4.
- **WA.** “Start a warranty claim from your order.” The only shared term is `a`. Score 1.

Ranking is SH, RF, WA. Predict the new result if a match on `refund` is worth four points instead of one. RF earns three extra points, so it takes first place with 7. SH stays at 6 and WA at 1.

**Actual LLM response.** Fictional fixture, “Contact us about your refund after 45 days,” after top-1 search sent only SH.

**Assessment.** Look at the words that earn SH points. It gets credit for both “45” and “after,” but those words describe a shipping delay. Open RF and read “within 30 days of purchase.” That line is the evidence the writer needed. Giving “refund” more weight changes which passage arrives first, without changing any policy wording.

**Failure classification.** Wrong subject first and required evidence cut from the writer’s context. Replay the weighting change on the same collection and include a shipping query before adopting it.

### Example 3. Hand-score two ranked lists

**User question.** Q1 asks about the refund window and proof. Q2 asks where to start a warranty claim.

**Approved reference.** The fictional reviewer gives Q1 `R` (complete refund rule) grade 2 and `M` (receipt help) grade 1. For Q2 only `W` (warranty claim instructions) earns grade 2. Every other item in these lists is judged 0. A positive grade counts as relevant for binary metrics.

- **Q1 ranks S, R, M, X.** The first slot has no relevant result, so Hit@1 is 0. Hit@3 is 1 because R and M appear inside three slots. Precision@3 is `2/3` and Recall@3 is `2/2`. R is the first relevant result at rank 2, giving reciprocal rank `1/2`.
- **Q2 ranks W, U, X.** W is the only relevant result and appears first, so Hit@1 and Hit@3 are both 1. Precision@3 is `1/3`, Recall@3 is `1/1`, and reciprocal rank is 1.

Across the two queries, mean Hit@1 is `(0 + 1)/2 = 0.5`. Mean Hit@3 and Recall@3 are both 1. Mean Precision@3 is `(2/3 + 1/3)/2 = 0.5`. MRR is `(1/2 + 1)/2 = 0.75`. For Q1, DCG@3 is `0 + 3/log₂(3) + 1/log₂(4) ≈ 2.393`. The ideal DCG is `3 + 1/log₂(3) ≈ 3.631`. Divide to get nDCG@3 ≈ 0.659. Q2 has its best passage first, so its nDCG@3 is 1.

**Actual LLM response.** Fictional Q1 fixture, “File a refund within 30 days of purchase and include a valid receipt,” after all three selected passages were delivered.

**Assessment.** At three slots Q1 can receive both relevant chunks despite the first result being wrong. Move M below the cutoff and Recall@3 drops to 1/2 for Q1, while the rank of R remains 2 and MRR stays 0.75.

**Failure classification.** Q1 has poor first-position retrieval. A stable MRR after moving M cannot tell you whether supporting material has been lost. Inspect coverage at the cutoff the writer actually uses, and verify the claims in its answer separately.

### Step 1 — Ingest documents

Check which policy records can enter the catalog. Python dictionaries store a name and value together. A list holds multiple records, and a set remembers IDs you have seen. The expression `key not in doc` detects a missing field before you try to read it. `any` is true as soon as one required key is missing. `continue` moves to the next record. The `f` in an f-string lets the braces insert values into the printed line.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
docs = [
    {"id": "RF", "title": "Refund eligibility", "source": "kb/refunds",
     "version": 2, "text": "Refund requests require a valid receipt."},
    {"id": "SH", "title": "Shipping delays", "source": "kb/shipping",
     "version": 1, "text": "Contact us after 45 days for shipping delays."},
]
add_duplicate = False
if add_duplicate:
    docs.append(docs[0])

required = ["id", "title", "source", "version", "text"]
seen = set()
for doc in docs:
    if any(key not in doc for key in required):
        print("Rejected: missing metadata")
        continue
    doc_id = doc["id"]
    if doc_id in seen:
        print(f"Rejected: duplicate {doc_id}")
        continue
    seen.add(doc_id)
    print(f"Loaded {doc_id} v{doc['version']} from {doc['source']}")
```

### Step 2 — Clean and tokenize text

Try matching a query to a policy sentence. `split()` without an argument cuts on whitespace and `' '.join(...)` puts one space between the pieces. `import re` makes Python’s pattern tools available. `re.findall` collects the pieces that match a pattern, and the `r` before the pattern keeps the backslashes available to those tools. The pattern reads runs of letters or numbers, allowing inward dots, slashes, or hyphens between digit groups to keep a decimal or a numeric date together. `&` takes the common items in two sets. `sorted` gives a predictable reading order.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
import re

def tokens(text):
    cleaned = " ".join(text.lower().split())
    return re.findall(r"[a-z]+|\d+(?:[./-]\d+)*", cleaned)

policy = "REFUND\twithin 30 days. Reference date: 2025-03-01."
query = "REFUND, 45 DAYS?"
print("Policy:", " ".join(tokens(policy)))
print("Query:", " ".join(tokens(query)))
print("Shared:", ", ".join(sorted(set(tokens(policy)) & set(tokens(query)))))
```

```text
Policy: refund within 30 days reference date 2025-03-01
Query: refund 45 days
Shared: days, refund
```

```text

Query: refund 30 days
Shared: 30, days, refund
```

Changing the number adds one shared token. Uppercase and punctuation around the words no longer matter, while the day number still does. This matcher can help diagnose a search. It cannot tell you whether a customer meets the deadline.

### Step 5 — Rank eligible chunks with rare-term weights

Compare refund and warranty passages using the simplified TF–IDF-style rule above. `sum(term in words for words in passages.values())` adds one per chunk containing a term. `math.log` computes the natural logarithm. The sorting function returns a tuple of two keys, putting larger scores first by negating them, then ordering equal scores by ID.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from math import log

texts = {
    "R": "Refund requests close within 30 days.",
    "W": "Warranty claims close after 60 days.",
}
add_shipping = False
if add_shipping:
    texts["S"] = "Ask shipping after 45 days."

def terms(text):
    return set(text.lower().replace(".", "").split())

passages = {key: terms(text) for key, text in texts.items()}
query = terms("refund after 45 days")
scores = {}
for key, words in passages.items():
    score = 0
    for term in sorted(query & words):
        df = sum(term in other for other in passages.values())
        score += 1 + log((len(passages) + 1) / (df + 1))
    if score:
        scores[key] = score

def sort_key(key):
    return (-scores[key], key)

for key in sorted(scores, key=sort_key):
    print(f"{key}: {scores[key]:.3f}")
```

```text
R: 2.405
W: 2.405
```

### Step 6 — Score two ranked lists

Reproduce Example 3. `enumerate(..., 1)` gives each passage its rank. `grades.get(chunk, 0)` returns the grade or zero because the exercise explicitly judges every unlisted passage irrelevant. `2 ** grade` raises 2 to the grade’s power. A list slice `[:k]` takes the first k items. `statistics.mean` averages one value per query. `next(..., 0)` takes the first matching reciprocal rank or uses zero. At the end, `zip(*rows)` groups the first value from each row, the second value from each row, and so on. `map(mean, ...)` averages each group. A zero grade would require an explicit judgment. In this fixed example, the `grades` dictionaries list only positive grades.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from math import log2
from statistics import mean

k = 3
ranking1 = ["S", "R", "M", "X"]
cases = [
    ("Q1", ranking1, {"R": 2, "M": 1}),
    ("Q2", ["W", "U", "X"], {"W": 2}),
]

def dcg(grades):
    return sum((2 ** grade - 1) / log2(rank + 1)
               for rank, grade in enumerate(grades, 1))

rows = []
for name, ranked, grades in cases:
    top_grades = [grades.get(chunk, 0) for chunk in ranked[:k]]
    hits = sum(grade > 0 for grade in top_grades)
    first_hit = int(bool(ranked) and ranked[0] in grades)
    hit = int(hits > 0)
    precision = hits / k
    recall = hits / len(grades)
    rr = next((1 / rank for rank, key in enumerate(ranked, 1) if key in grades), 0)
    ideal = dcg(sorted(grades.values(), reverse=True)[:k])
    ndcg = dcg(top_grades) / ideal
    rows.append((first_hit, hit, precision, recall, rr, ndcg))
    print(f"{name}: H@1={first_hit} H@3={hit} P@3={precision:.3f} R@3={recall:.3f} RR={rr:.3f} nDCG@3={ndcg:.3f}")

h1, h3, p, r, mrr, ndcg = map(mean, zip(*rows))
print(f"Mean: H@1={h1:.3f} H@3={h3:.3f} P@3={p:.3f} R@3={r:.3f} MRR={mrr:.3f} nDCG@3={ndcg:.3f}")
```

```text
Q1: H@1=0 H@3=1 P@3=0.667 R@3=1.000 RR=0.500 nDCG@3=0.659
Q2: H@1=1 H@3=1 P@3=0.333 R@3=1.000 RR=1.000 nDCG@3=1.000
Mean: H@1=0.500 H@3=1.000 P@3=0.500 R@3=1.000 MRR=0.750 nDCG@3=0.830
```

             "Reset your password from the sign-in page."),
    document("ES", "Escalations", "kb/escalate", 1,
             "Send unresolved shipping issues to Support."),
]
published = {
    "kb/refunds": 2, "kb/internal": 1, "kb/shipping": 1,
    "kb/warranty": 1, "kb/receipts": 1, "kb/damage": 1,
    "kb/account": 1, "kb/escalate": 1,
}

    ("Q1", "Can I get a refund after 45 days without a receipt?", {"RF-v2:s01"}),
    ("Q2", "damaged product refund claim", {"RF-v2:s02", "RF-v2:s03"}),
    ("Q3", "missing receipt", {"MR-v1:s01"}),
    ("Q4", "warranty claim", {"WA-v1:s01"}),
    ("Q5", "retired loyalty discount", set()),
]

def validate(documents):
    seen = set()
    for row in documents:
        for field in ("id", "title", "source", "text", "status", "role"):
            if not isinstance(row.get(field), str) or not row[field].strip():
                raise ValueError(f"Missing or blank {field}")
        if type(row.get("version")) is not int or row["version"] < 1:
            raise ValueError("Version must be a positive integer")
        if row["status"] not in {"published", "archived"} or row["role"] not in {"public", "staff"}:
            raise ValueError("Unknown status or role")
        if row["id"] in seen:
            raise ValueError(f"Duplicate ID {row['id']}")
        seen.add(row["id"])

def terms(text):
    return set(re.findall(r"[a-z]+|\d+(?:[./-]\d+)*", text.lower()))

def build(documents, versions, roles, size, overlap):
    validate(documents)
    if type(size) is not int or type(overlap) is not int or size < 1 or not 0 <= overlap < size:
        raise ValueError("Use a positive size and overlap from zero to size minus one")
    chunks, index, fingerprints = {}, {}, set()
    for row in sorted(documents, key=lambda doc: doc["id"]):
        if row["role"] not in roles or row["status"] != "published":
            continue
        if row["version"] != versions.get(row["source"]):
            continue
        prefix = f"{row['id']}-v{row['version']}"
        sentences = re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", " ".join(row["text"].split()))
        for number, start in enumerate(range(0, len(sentences), size - overlap), 1):
            end = min(start + size, len(sentences))
            body = " ".join(sentences[start:end])
            fingerprint = (row["source"], row["version"], row["role"], body.casefold())
            if fingerprint not in fingerprints:
                fingerprints.add(fingerprint)
                key = f"{prefix}:c{number:02d}"
                chunks[key] = {
                    "doc": row["id"], "title": row["title"], "source": row["source"],
                    "version": row["version"], "role": row["role"], "text": body,
                    "spans": {f"{prefix}:s{i + 1:02d}" for i in range(start, end)},
                }
                for term in terms(body):
                    index.setdefault(term, set()).add(key)
            if end == len(sentences):
                break
    return chunks, index

def rank(query, index, weight):
    scores = {}
    for term in terms(query):
        for key in index.get(term, set()):
            scores[key] = scores.get(key, 0) + (weight if term == "refund" else 1)
    return sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda row: (-row[1], row[0]))

def assess(ranked, chunks, gold, cutoff):
    if type(cutoff) is not int or cutoff < 1:
        raise ValueError("Use a positive cutoff")
    top = ranked[:cutoff]
    relevant = {key for key, chunk in chunks.items() if chunk["spans"] & gold}
    if not gold:
        return None, "REVIEW: no positive gold; check answerability separately"
    if not relevant:
        return None, "FAIL: known evidence absent from the eligible chunks"
    chosen = {key for key, _ in top}
    hits = len(chosen & relevant)
    evidence = set()
    for key in chosen:
        evidence.update(chunks[key]["spans"] & gold)
    rr = next((1 / i for i, (key, _) in enumerate(ranked, 1) if key in relevant), 0)
    metrics = (int(hits > 0), hits / cutoff, hits / len(relevant), rr)
    if not evidence:
        decision = "FAIL: required evidence absent from selection"
    elif evidence != gold:
        decision = "REVIEW: only part of the required evidence selected"
    elif rr != 1:
        decision = "REVIEW: full evidence selected but first relevant chunk ranks below 1"
    else:
        decision = "PASS: full evidence selected and relevant chunk ranks first"
    return metrics, decision

public_roles = {"public"}
chunks, index = build(docs, published, public_roles, *layout)
print(f"Northstar kb-v2 | gold-v1 | public | size/overlap={layout} | k={k} | refund weight={refund_weight}")
print(f"Indexed {len(chunks)} chunks")
for key, chunk in chunks.items():

rows = []
for query_id, query, gold in queries:

    metrics, decision = assess(ranked, chunks, gold, k)
    evidence = ", ".join(sorted(gold)) or "none"
    ranking = ", ".join(f"{key}={score}" for key, score in ranked) or "none"
    selected = ", ".join(key for key, _ in ranked[:k]) or "none"
    print(f"{query_id}: {query}")
    print(f"  Gold: {evidence} | Ranked: {ranking} | Selected: {selected}")
    if metrics is None:

        rows.append(metrics)

else:
    print(f"Scored 0/{len(queries)} | Metrics N/A")

## Common Mistakes

- Scoring results before verifying who can access their text. Filter by approved version and access before building the rankable list.
- Checking the ranked list without checking the exact context sent for the answer. A retrieved passage can be cut by top-k or by a later context limit.
- Comparing chunk recall across builds without remapping the gold spans. The number of relevant chunks can change even when the underlying policy does not.
- Treating unjudged passages as confirmed irrelevant or counting an empty gold set as zero recall. Show what was judged and how many queries were scored.
- Increasing k or overlap without measuring cost. Read which passages were added and count repeated text and input space used.

## Lesson Summary and Next Step

Ingestion keeps policy text and its origin available for review. Chunking decides which parts can travel together. The index finds lexical candidates and ranking decides which ones fit into the supplied context. Gold spans show whether that context covers the actual policy, and rank metrics tell you which parts appeared early. Use the raw evidence to explain the defect before changing the system.
