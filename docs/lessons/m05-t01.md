The second shares only two. If you ranked them by the number of matching words, which would win? Keep that ranking beside your own reading of the policy. We'll work out why they disagree.

## Beginner Explanation

### Count what the two lines share

Put the approved line above the draft “fee fee applies.” Cross off the first “fee” in each line. When you reach the draft’s second “fee,” there’s no uncrossed “fee” left on the approved line. Cross off “applies” in both lines and you have two matches. That limit on how often a repeated word can count is called clipping.

The bank has written a short line that is acceptable under the approved policy. That line is the reference. The reply we want to score is the candidate. In this lesson every piece between spaces is a token. That's a convenient way of splitting these lowercase examples. A shared scoring tool might split punctuation differently, so record how you split the text when comparing scores.

Now look at “monthly fee applies.” All three of its tokens appear in the five-token reference. Token precision is the fraction of candidate tokens that match, so precision is 3 ÷ 3 = 1. Token recall is the fraction of reference tokens recovered, so recall is 3 ÷ 5 = 0.60. The reply gets full precision even though it leaves out the condition customers need.

F1 balances those two fractions. With the same clipped match count on both sides, double the three matches and divide by the total length of the two lines: 6 ÷ (3 + 5) = 0.75. This is the usual F1 calculation written using counts. That 0.75 is a text overlap score. It gives no special weight to the missing cutoff. Keep both precision and recall visible during review. A reply that contains every approved word and three extra words also gets a similar F1, 10 ÷ 13 ≈ 0.77. You’d investigate the missing condition in the first case and the added wording in the second.

### Compare neighbors and words in order

Try a draft that puts the first two words in the opposite order, “fee monthly applies below 5000.” You still have all five words, but “monthly fee” is gone as a pair. Count one word at a time and you get all five matches. Slide a two-word window along each line and you find only two shared pairs, “applies below” and “below 5000.” A neighboring run of `n` tokens is an n-gram. A one-token run is a unigram and a two-token run is a bigram.

ROUGE-N compares n-gram overlap against a reference. For instance ROUGE-2 counts bigrams. Reports can include precision, recall, and F1, so write down which one you used. Both lines above contain four pairs, and two match. The ROUGE-2 precision and recall are each 2 ÷ 4, so the F1 is 0.50. ROUGE-1 F1 is still 1.00. [Original ROUGE paper](https://aclanthology.org/W04-1013/)

Try removing “applies” instead. The line “monthly fee below 5000” shares four words that stay in the same order, even though the reference has a gap between “fee” and “below.” The longest list you can trace in both lines while preserving order is the longest common subsequence. ROUGE-L uses its length. Here its F1 is 2 × 4 ÷ (4 + 5) = 0.889. The gap is allowed, but reversing the line would prevent those same four words from all staying in order. When shared wording matters, the two methods tell you different things about the change. [Original ROUGE paper](https://aclanthology.org/W04-1013/)

### Ask whether a reply got a high score by saying very little

“Monthly fee” has perfect one-word and two-word precision against the reference. It could earn full credit if you only considered the words it used. Yet a customer still wouldn't know when they pay. BLEU measures clipped n-gram precision across several lengths and reduces the score when the candidate is too short compared with the reference. This multiplier is the brevity penalty. If the candidate is two tokens and the reference is five, the multiplier is about 0.22. With perfect precision for the lengths we count, a teaching BLEU-2 score on a 0–100 scale falls from 100 to about 22.

For BLEU-2, multiply the one-word precision by the two-word precision and take the square root. For example, with 0.80 and 0.75, multiply to get 0.60, then take the square root to get about 0.775. This is the geometric mean. BLEU-4 does the same with four precisions and a fourth root. Multiply by the brevity penalty, then by 100 for the scale used in this chapter.

A sentence can share several individual words and still share zero pairs. The product then becomes zero. Some settings use a small substitute for an observed zero precision to make short sentences easier to compare. This is smoothing. The first missing order under the library setting used here counts as half a match for calculation purposes. The next missing order counts as a quarter. Keep the raw count visible so nobody mistakes a substitute for a match that occurred. [Original BLEU paper](https://aclanthology.org/P02-1040/) · [SacreBLEU implementation](https://github.com/mjpost/sacrebleu/blob/v2.5.1/sacrebleu/metrics/bleu.py)

If you score two replies separately, then average their scores, each reply gets equal weight. Corpus BLEU, calculated over the whole set, adds match counts and possible counts first. It uses the total candidate and reference lengths for the brevity penalty. A five-word reply can contribute more possible pairs than a two-word reply. Record whether you used corpus BLEU or the mean of sentence BLEU before comparing reports.

### Allow more than one acceptable wording

Ask two policy reviewers to write a short summary independently. If both summaries preserve the same rule, you can approve both and keep them with the benchmark. With multiple references, BLEU clips each n-gram against the largest count seen in any one reference and uses the closest reference length, preferring the shorter length on a tie. In `rouge-score`, the multiple-reference operation selects the best F1 separately for each requested metric. Our token F1 report similarly takes the best score against an individual approved line. Joining the approved lines into one long reference would describe a different scoring task. [SacreBLEU implementation](https://github.com/mjpost/sacrebleu/blob/v2.5.1/sacrebleu/metrics/bleu.py) · [Google Research rouge-score](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/blob/master/rouge/rouge_scorer.py)

If you need to compare close spellings or forms of the same word, you can also count neighboring characters. For “fee,” the two-character pieces are “fe” and “ee.” “Fees” adds “es” while keeping both pieces. chrF is an optional metric that compares character runs of several lengths using precision and recall. It can give partial credit where whole words differ. Similar spellings can still express different facts. [Original chrF paper](https://aclanthology.org/W15-3049/)

Freeze the input, references, scoring options and implementation version before comparing two releases. When a review adds new acceptable wording, record that as a new reference version and rescore both releases with it. If both of the assistant’s replies are unchanged, a higher score after that edit tells you the scoring rule changed. To see whether a new assistant improved, first score both versions against the same approved references, then review the replies against the policy.

### Example 1. A reply that leaves out the condition

**User question.** When does the Standard Savings monthly fee apply?

**Approved reference.** The July policy says “monthly fee applies below 5000.”

**Actual LLM response.** The fictional saved reply is “monthly fee applies.”

**Assessment.** First write down the three matching words. All three candidate words match, giving 3 ÷ 3 = 1.00 precision. Only three of the five reference words match, giving 3 ÷ 5 = 0.60 recall. F1 is 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75. Read the reply as a customer and try to decide whether a balance of 4900 incurs the fee. The reply does not give you enough information.

**Failure classification.** Mark the reply incomplete based on the policy question. Use the recall result to flag it for inspection. A low recall score alone would also flag some valid paraphrases, so use the policy itself to decide which information is missing.

### Example 2. Same words, different order

**User question.** Write a clean one-line summary of the July fee trigger.

**Approved reference.** “monthly fee applies below 5000.”

**Actual LLM response.** The fictional saved reply is “fee monthly applies below 5000.”

**Assessment.** Underline the matching words first. All five match. Next list the four candidate pairs: “fee monthly,” “monthly applies,” “applies below,” and “below 5000.” Only the last two appear as pairs in the reference. That gives ROUGE-2 F1 of 0.50. You can keep “monthly applies below 5000” in order in both lines, so the longest common subsequence has four tokens. ROUGE-L F1 is 8 ÷ 10 = 0.80.

**Failure classification.** Send the line back for a wording edit. The awkward order broke two pairs, but you can still read the cutoff as 5000. If a second draft scored just as low, you would still read it against the policy before deciding whether to fix its wording or correct a factual error.

### Example 3. The scoring winner gets the policy wrong

**User question.** Summarize when a Standard Savings customer pays the monthly fee.

**Approved reference.** Keep the July rule fixed: “monthly fee applies below 5000.” The approved evidence says an exact balance of 5000 does not trigger the fee.

**Actual LLM response.** Two fictional saved replies are A, “balances under 5000 incur a monthly charge,” and B, “monthly fee applies below 6000.”

**Assessment.** Score both against the same approved line. A shares two tokens, giving F1 of 4 ÷ 12 = 0.3333. B shares four and gives 8 ÷ 10 = 0.8000. B also leads on bigram and in-order overlap. Now test the claim at a balance of 5500. B says a fee applies there. The July rule says it does not. A preserves the correct rule.

**Failure classification.** Record a wrong cutoff for B. Accept A on the supplied policy criterion and keep B as a case to inspect whenever a scoring rule changes. The evidence covers these two examples. It does not tell you how often the assistant gets the policy wrong.

### Step 1 — Count repeated words fairly

Check how often a reply repeats “fee.” `split()` turns each line into a list of words. `Counter` (included with Python) counts how many times each word occurs. Putting `&` between two counters keeps the smaller count for each shared word. `values()` gets just the counts, and `sum()` adds them. The `from ... import ...` line makes `Counter` available in this program.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python

candidate = "fee fee applies"
counts = Counter(candidate.split()) & Counter(reference.split())

print("Matched:", sum(counts.values()))
print("Candidate tokens:", len(candidate.split()))
```

```text
Candidate tokens: 3
```

### Step 3 — Compare neighboring words

Score Example 2 by sliding a window along the words. `words[start:start + size]` takes the words from `start` up to the position before `start + size`. `range(len(words) - size + 1)` gives every starting position where a full window fits. `tuple()` makes an ordered group that a `Counter` can use as a key. In the loop, `sequence` takes the values 1 and 2 in turn. `def` gives the window-counting steps the name `runs`, and `return` passes the finished count back. `counts[group] += 1` adds one for the current window.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from collections import Counter

def runs(words, size):
    counts = Counter()
    for start in range(len(words) - size + 1):
        group = tuple(words[start:start + size])
        counts[group] += 1
    return counts

reference = "monthly fee applies below 5000".split()
candidate = "fee monthly applies below 5000".split()
for sequence in (1, 2):
    expected = runs(reference, sequence)
    actual = runs(candidate, sequence)
    matched = sum((actual & expected).values())
    total = sum(actual.values()) + sum(expected.values())
    print(f"ROUGE-{sequence} F1: {2 * matched / total:.2f}")
```

```text
ROUGE-1 F1: 1.00
ROUGE-2 F1: 0.50
```

**Experiment.** Change just the candidate to `monthly fee applies below 5000`, keeping `.split()`.

```text

ROUGE-2 F1: 1.00
```

### Step 4 — Keep words in order across a gap

Follow “monthly” with your finger in both lines. In “monthly fee” the next word is “fee.” In “monthly below fee” you have to move past “below” to reach it. Your fingers moved forward in both lines, so the shared sequence still has two words. The program keeps track of how far that kind of match has gone as it reads longer beginnings of each line.

In the function, one row of numbers records the best length so far for each beginning of `right`. `enumerate(right, 1)` supplies each word and its position starting at one. A matching word extends the answer for the two shorter beginnings by one, using `previous[j - 1]`. If words differ, `max()` chooses the better result from dropping one end word or the other. `current[-1]` means the last count built for this row, and `append()` adds a new count to it.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
def shared_order(left, right):
    previous = [0] * (len(right) + 1)
    for word in left:
        current = [0]
        for j, other in enumerate(right, 1):
            if word == other:
                length = previous[j - 1] + 1
            else:
                length = max(previous[j], current[-1])
            current.append(length)
        previous = current
    return previous[-1]

reference = "monthly fee applies below 5000".split()
candidate = "monthly fee below 5000".split()
length = shared_order(candidate, reference)
print("Shared in order:", length)
print(f"ROUGE-L F1: {2 * length / (len(candidate) + len(reference)):.4f}")
```

```text
Shared in order: 4
ROUGE-L F1: 0.8889
```

**Experiment.** Change only the candidate text to `5000 below fee monthly`. The words are the same as before.


### Step 5 — See how BLEU treats short and unusual replies

Try a reply with four of the five reference words and no matching pairs. The scoring example uses up to two words at a time, so this is BLEU-2. `zip(words, words[1:])` lines up each word with its next neighbor. `sqrt()` takes a square root. `exp(x)` raises a fixed mathematical number, about 2.718, to the power `x`. For a short reply, the multiplier is `exp(1 - reference length / candidate length)`. With four versus five words, its input is −0.25, giving about 0.7788. Replies at least as long as the reference use a multiplier of 1. In the code, `min(0, ...)` keeps that exponent at or below zero. The `if` line changes the pair count used in the calculation only when smoothing is on and there were zero shared pairs.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from collections import Counter
from math import exp, sqrt

reference = "monthly fee applies below 5000".split()
candidate = "fee monthly below applies".split()
use_smoothing = False

one = sum((Counter(candidate) & Counter(reference)).values())
pairs = Counter(zip(candidate, candidate[1:]))
ref_pairs = Counter(zip(reference, reference[1:]))
two = sum((pairs & ref_pairs).values())
pair_count = sum(pairs.values())
adjusted = two
if use_smoothing and two == 0:
    adjusted = 0.5
penalty = exp(min(0, 1 - len(reference) / len(candidate)))
score = 100 * penalty * sqrt((one / len(candidate)) * (adjusted / pair_count))
print(f"Word matches: {one}/{len(candidate)}")
print(f"Pair matches: {two}/{pair_count}")
print(f"Brevity multiplier: {penalty:.4f}")
print(f"BLEU-2: {score:.2f}")
```

```text
Word matches: 4/4
Pair matches: 0/3
Brevity multiplier: 0.7788
BLEU-2: 0.00
```

The score was zero because the two-word precision was zero. With smoothing, the calculation uses 0.5 ÷ 3 in its place. ### Step 6 — Score a set instead of averaging replies

Score a short reply and a longer reply separately, then score the same pair as a collection. `stats()` returns five values in a fixed order. The names on the left of `=` receive those values in that same order. When a score is calculated for the whole collection, `sum(row[i] for row in rows)` adds the same field from every row. `score(*totals)` passes the five values from `totals` as five inputs to `score`. `+=` adds a number to a running total. The expression in square brackets repeats the sum once per field. `rows[0]` is the first row, which tells Python how many fields to add.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from collections import Counter
from math import exp, sqrt

def stats(candidate, reference):
    words = sum((Counter(candidate) & Counter(reference)).values())
    pairs = Counter(zip(candidate, candidate[1:]))
    ref_pairs = Counter(zip(reference, reference[1:]))
    pair_matches = sum((pairs & ref_pairs).values())
    return len(candidate), len(reference), words, pair_matches, sum(pairs.values())

def score(length, ref_length, words, pair_matches, pairs):
    penalty = exp(min(0, 1 - ref_length / length))
    return 100 * penalty * sqrt((words / length) * (pair_matches / pairs))

reference = "monthly fee applies below 5000".split()
candidates = ["monthly fee", "monthly fee applies below 6000"]
rows = []
score_sum = 0
for number, text in enumerate(candidates, 1):
    row = stats(text.split(), reference)
    rows.append(row)
    value = score(*row)
    score_sum += value
    print(f"Reply {number}: {value:.2f}")

totals = [sum(row[i] for row in rows) for i in range(len(rows[0]))]
print(f"Mean of reply scores: {score_sum / len(rows):.2f}")
print(f"Collection score: {score(*totals):.2f}")
```

```text
Reply 1: 22.31
Reply 2: 77.46
Mean of reply scores: 49.89
Collection score: 53.94
```

**Experiment.** In `candidates`, change `monthly fee applies below 6000` to `monthly fee applies below 5000`. Leave the first reply as it is.

```text

Reply 2: 100.00
Mean of reply scores: 61.16
Collection score: 65.14
```

In the first run, the collection has six matching words out of seven and four matching pairs out of five. Its length multiplier uses seven candidate words and ten reference words. In the second run, the longer reply gains a word match and a pair match. It keeps its length, so the multiplier stays the same. **Checkpoint.** Why did the collection's score differ from the average? The collection combines counts and lengths before applying the score. Confirm which calculation a report used before investigating a difference between reports. Both calculations can hide a wrong cutoff in a single reply.

### Step 7 — Compare two replies against approved wording

Put the measurements beside a reviewer's policy verdict. The programs above count shared words and runs. This one uses those same operations and also reads a label supplied by a reviewer who checked the cutoff independently. Both replies and labels are fictional. Both starting examples have enough words to count up to four at a time. The assignment also tries a shorter reply. The BLEU calculation follows the library settings described below for these inputs, and the ROUGE calculations report F1.

`Counter()` starts an empty count. `caps |= runs(reference, size)` keeps the largest count for each run across the approved references. `min(..., key=...)` chooses a length by the rule supplied in `key`. The `lambda` is a short, unnamed function. It returns two numbers to compare in order. The first is the distance from the candidate’s length, which `abs()` makes positive or zero. The second is the length itself, so a tie goes to the shorter one. `prod()` multiplies the precisions, and `**` raises the product to the power that gives the requested root. Four precisions give a power of one-quarter. `smoothing *= 2` doubles the divisor at each missing order. The loop returns zero if even the single words have no matches. In the print string, `:g` drops unnecessary decimal zeros, and `.4f` prints four decimal places.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
from collections import Counter
from math import exp, prod

def runs(words, size):
    return Counter(tuple(words[i:i + size])
                   for i in range(len(words) - size + 1))

def run_f1(candidate, reference, size):
    actual = runs(candidate, size)
    expected = runs(reference, size)
    matched = sum((actual & expected).values())
    return 2 * matched / (sum(actual.values()) + sum(expected.values()))

def ordered_f1(candidate, reference):
    row = [0] * (len(reference) + 1)
    for word in candidate:
        next_row = [0]
        for j, other in enumerate(reference, 1):
            if word == other:
                next_row.append(row[j - 1] + 1)
            else:
                next_row.append(max(row[j], next_row[-1]))
        row = next_row
    return 2 * row[-1] / (len(candidate) + len(reference))

def bleu4(candidate, references):
    length = len(candidate)
    lengths = [len(reference) for reference in references]
    closest = min(lengths, key=lambda size: (abs(size - length), size))
    multiplier = exp(min(0, 1 - closest / length))
    smoothing = 1
    precisions = []
    for size in range(1, min(4, length) + 1):
        actual = runs(candidate, size)
        caps = Counter()
        for reference in references:
            caps |= runs(reference, size)
        matches = sum((actual & caps).values())
        if matches == 0 and size == 1:
            return 0.0
        if matches == 0:
            smoothing *= 2
            matches = 1 / smoothing
        precisions.append(matches / sum(actual.values()))
    return 100 * multiplier * prod(precisions) ** (1 / len(precisions))

first = "monthly fee applies below 5000".split()
second = "balances under 5000 incur a monthly charge".split()
use_second_reference = False
references = [first]
if use_second_reference:
    references.append(second)

replies = [
    ("A", "balances under 5000 incur a monthly charge", "CORRECT"),
    ("B", "monthly fee applies below 6000", "WRONG CUTOFF"),
]
print("Approved wordings:", len(references))
for name, text, policy_label in replies:
    candidate = text.split()
    token = max(run_f1(candidate, ref, 1) for ref in references)
    rouge2 = max(run_f1(candidate, ref, 2) for ref in references)
    rouge_l = max(ordered_f1(candidate, ref) for ref in references)
    bleu = bleu4(candidate, references)
    print(f"{name} | token F1 {token:.4f} | BLEU {bleu:.4f}")
    print(f"  ROUGE-2 {rouge2:.4f} | ROUGE-L {rouge_l:.4f} | {policy_label}")
```

```text
Approved wordings: 1
A | token F1 0.3333 | BLEU 7.8098
  ROUGE-2 0.0000 | ROUGE-L 0.1667 | CORRECT
B | token F1 0.8000 | BLEU 66.8740
  ROUGE-2 0.7500 | ROUGE-L 0.8000 | WRONG CUTOFF
```

With one reference, B leads in all four columns, but a reviewer comparing the meaning with the policy sees the changed cutoff. The program prints that judgment from the label supplied with each reply.

#### Compare with the library definitions

Keep the July reference and both summaries exactly as printed. For SacreBLEU, the comparable configuration is case sensitive, with tokenizer `none` (use the supplied spaces), smoothing method `exp`, maximum order 4 and effective order enabled. Effective order stops at the number of token positions a short candidate actually has. With these first two candidates, orders one through four are available. 2. Check recall and then the meaning of the missing words. The short reply in Example 1 omits the cutoff even though every word it uses matches.

5. An average gives each reply one vote. The collection score combines length and match counts first, so the longer reply contributes more possible words and pairs.

## Common Mistakes

A percentage-looking score can hide a serious error. Before clearing a draft, check decision-critical details such as the amount, which account it applies to, and what happens at the boundary.

A high precision with low recall can mean a reply left something out. It may also mean the approved line and the reply used different words. Read the pair before treating a recall drop as an omission.

BLEU can change when smoothing, tokenization, or aggregation changes. Keep the raw counts and the settings beside a score. If a report omits those details, rerun the comparison under one shared setup.

## Lesson Summary and Next Step

Start with the approved evidence. Token F1 measures shared counts, ROUGE-2 measures neighboring pairs, ROUGE-L follows shared words in order, and BLEU combines clipped precision at different lengths with a penalty for short answers. Use these measurements to flag a difference worth reading. Record which replies, references, settings and scale you used before comparing results. When a customer's decision depends on a fact such as a cutoff, check the fact independently.
