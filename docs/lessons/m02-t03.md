## Beginner Explanation

### 1. Try the question away from customer traffic

Take the ATM question from the opening. You can put it on a sheet with the policy the assistant received and both saved answers. Read the replies at your desk and check which one makes a claim the policy can support. No customer is waiting while you look. Checking saved replies or running test questions outside live serving is **offline evaluation**.

Save the question, the date or account, the approved evidence, and what the reply should do. A **reference answer** shows one reply you’d accept. A **rubric** tells the reviewer what to look for. In a fee case, “A monthly fee of ₹100 applies” and “The charge is ₹100 per month” express the same amount and timing. Your rubric can accept both.

Harbor Leaf’s examples reuse these approved policy excerpts. The symbol §2 means section 2.

- **SV-1 §2.** From January 1 through June 30, 2026, inclusive, the Standard Savings minimum was ₹4,000. A ₹80 monthly fee applied below ₹4,000.
- **SV-2 §2.** From July 1, 2026, the minimum is ₹5,000. A ₹100 monthly fee applies below ₹5,000. The below-minimum fee does not apply at exactly ₹5,000. The excerpt gives no ATM limit.

Ask what applies on June 15. Open SV-1 even if the customer happens to ask in July. Now ask the bank for an ATM limit using only SV-2. Can you find a number in that excerpt? No. An acceptable reply says it can’t confirm the limit. Holding back a claim you can’t support is called **abstention**.

Suppose a reviewer says “a ₹100 fee every day” should have passed. Start by checking the same saved reply against the same policy. Later, you could send that saved question to a new version in a test environment and capture a fresh reply. Using saved inputs or outputs to repeat a check during development is **development replay**. The lab uses replies already written into the examples.

The same saved text and fixed rule let you reproduce a result. If you ask a model to generate the reply again, the words or answer may change. Save the version, settings, and replies so you know what was actually checked. Run comparable trials more than once if that variation could affect your decision.

Offline evaluation can catch mistakes before customers see them and help you reproduce a failure while you change one component. It works without customer traffic and lets you include rare but severe failures deliberately. Its coverage ends where its cases end. Passing six saved examples says what happened on those six examples. It gives little evidence about the frequency of errors across live traffic.

### 2. See what happens when customers use it

The bank lets a small group of customers use the repaired assistant. One asks about a fee at an exact balance on the last day of June. Another cares more about how long it takes to get any reply. The live service customers use is called **production**. Checking what happens during live use is **online evaluation**. Scoring those same replies later from a saved batch is offline scoring of records collected online.

Say the bank logs when each question arrives and when the reply finishes. The elapsed time is **latency**. Use the same start and end points when comparing versions. Keeping track of such measurements over time is **monitoring**. The bank can also track spending and ask reviewers to check some replies for incorrect policy claims. Each measure answers its own question.

If there are too many replies to read, choose some to review. That smaller group is a **sample**. You might select replies at random throughout the day and separately pull extra historical-policy questions because they’re rare and important.

Keep those groups separate in your report or account for how you chose them when estimating a rate. Reading a pile of complaints can help you find a problem. Those complaints alone tell you little about how often the whole customer base sees it.

A customer taps thumbs-up on a reply that tells them they owed a fee. That’s **user feedback**. It tells the team the customer gave a positive rating. To check the amount, separately look up the rule that applied on the date they asked about. A quick, confident reply can earn a thumbs-up while giving the wrong fee.

When you report a fraction, say what you divided by. That’s the **denominator**. For example, “4 of 12 submitted ratings were negative” and “2 of 30 reviewed replies failed the policy check” answer different questions. People who didn’t rate their reply may have had a different experience.

Suppose you’re checking slow replies. Do you need the customer’s message? Time taken, the assistant version, the question category, and the source version may already help you check slow responses and old evidence. For a policy error, use a narrowly chosen example with identifiers removed, or build a fictional example that produces the same error. Follow the bank’s rules for approval, who can see the records, and how long to keep them. Keep real messages, PINs, and account numbers out of the lab.

If unsupported advice reaches a customer and could cause harm, the team can treat it as a **safety incident**. Follow the bank’s incident process and keep only what’s needed to investigate.

### 3. Match the evidence to the decision

- **Inputs.** Offline evaluation uses saved cases, expectations, and approved evidence. Online evaluation uses deployed behavior such as sampled replies, timing, spend, and feedback.
- **Strengths.** Offline evaluation checks the same known cases before release and helps isolate changes. Online evaluation reveals unexpected questions and live traffic or system conditions.
- **Limits.** Offline results can be misleading when coverage is narrow or the team has tuned on the test cases. Online results can be misleading when samples are unrepresentative, labels are missing, or traffic differs between versions.
- **Decisions.** An offline gate can stop a candidate that guesses an ATM limit on a known case. Online evidence about a new policy error can justify pausing a rollout already in progress.

The new version you’re deciding whether to release is the **candidate**. Suppose the June question reaches the old version and the customer sees its reply. The bank sends an approved copy of that same request to the candidate, whose answer only reviewers see. That’s **shadow evaluation**. You can compare the text each version would give. The customer only saw one of them, so their rating belongs to that reply. Shadow requests still cost money and can contain sensitive information. Follow the bank’s approval rules and keep the candidate’s work separate from customer-facing actions.

Now let a small, controlled share of eligible requests receive the candidate’s actual reply. That’s a **canary**, a limited rollout the team can pause if it sees harm. If this group asks more historical questions than last week’s customers, a higher failure rate could reflect that difference. Check which questions each group asked before attributing the result to the version.

Say you want to know whether the new version actually changes how many customers get correct answers. Set up a comparison before looking at the results. In an **online experiment**, you decide what to change and compare groups that experience different versions.

In an **A/B test**, a planned rule assigns eligible users to one of two versions. Assigning them at random during the same time window helps balance the kinds of customers and questions across groups. For this comparison, keep the eligibility rules and the way you judge replies the same. Decide which outcome to measure, how long to measure, and when to stop if you see harm. With a sound design and enough evidence, that comparison can help estimate what the version changed.

Replaying saved live requests offline can reproduce a problem. To estimate what caused a change in live user behavior, you need evidence suited to that question.

### 4. Follow the evaluation lifecycle

How does one wrong fee answer change what you do next week? Start with the reason you’re checking it. Then use the results of the test and the rollout to improve the next round. Those connected steps are the **evaluation lifecycle**. Follow the fee question through them.

1. **Define objectives and success criteria.** Ask what decision the evaluation supports. The bank wants July fee answers to give the correct amount and frequency and unsupported financial questions to receive a safe abstention. Decide in advance what evidence would be enough for the release decision.
2. **Prepare representative cases.** Include common questions, wording variants, boundaries, older policy dates, and unsupported questions. Give each case an ID, evidence, and expected behavior. Separate a development set from cases reserved to assess the release so that tuning on known answers does not inflate confidence.
3. **Establish a baseline.** Record the starting version and its results. For Harbor Leaf, use the currently deployed prompt with its model and approved sources. A **prompt** is the instructions and other input supplied to the model. The version being considered is the **candidate**.
4. **Run offline evaluations.** Score each version on the same saved cases with the same evidence and rubric. Record the replies and judgments beside their case IDs. Keep a separate outcome for unresolved evidence or an execution error. A request that never produced a reply supplies no answer to score.
5. **Analyze failures.** Group results by what went wrong, such as “wrong fee frequency” or “unsupported limit.” Then investigate why. Read the question, source excerpts, instructions, and reply. The same wrong charge can come from the wrong policy being supplied or from the assistant misreading a correct one.
6. **Improve one part of the system deliberately.** You might clarify the prompt, fix **retrieval** (the step that finds and supplies documents), or change the model's configuration. Log the change and the reason for it. A better retrieval result will not help if the assistant ignores the effective date in that result.
7. **Re-evaluate for regressions.** A **regression** is behavior that gets worse after a change. Pair baseline and candidate results for each case, including ones the baseline already passed. Keep known failure cases in a **regression suite**, a reusable set of tests that guards against mistakes returning.
8. **Make a release decision.** Check the release criteria, list remaining risks, and record whether you would approve a monitored release, block it, or gather more evidence. Name the owner and the condition that would pause or reverse the rollout.
9. **Monitor online behavior.** Review results from the approved rollout. Track correctness where you have evidence, response latency and cost, safety events, and feedback. Define when a team member should look more closely and when the rollout must pause.
10. **Investigate incidents and update the dataset.** Contain any harmful behavior, then reproduce it safely. Add an evidence-backed case that would have caught it. Save the old benchmark and run. Start a new benchmark version and score both versions on it before claiming a comparative improvement.

Think of the handoff as a loop. Save a case, use it to test a change, decide whether to release, watch what happens, and turn a new failure into another saved case.

For your decision notes, start with the decision and write down the assistant versions and the version of the saved questions and scoring rules. Keep the reviewed and unresolved counts, changed cases, safety findings, and your recommendation together. Write who should act next. If the evidence comes from live use, add when you measured, which requests you looked at, how you chose them, what you counted, and any limits on what you could collect.

### 5. Apply release gates without losing individual failures

Suppose you checked seven replies. Five pass, one fails, and one still needs a decision. If you report “five out of six,” say that you counted only the replies you could decide. That fraction is the **pass rate** among decided cases. Also report that you reached a decision on six out of seven. Otherwise the missing judgment disappears from the report.

Next ask what went wrong. “Wrong fee frequency” is a **failure category**, a name for the kind of mistake you saw. Some mistakes have consequences the team regards as unacceptable. We call those failures **safety-critical**. In this lesson that includes making up an ATM limit when the approved evidence doesn’t give one.

Before looking at a new score, agree on what would be enough to proceed. The rules you agree to use are your **release gate**. Here are the invented rules for the lab.

1. **BLOCK** if any confirmed candidate safety failure exists.
2. **INVESTIGATE** if a required candidate case remains under review after checking for confirmed safety failures.
3. **BLOCK** if less than 80% of all required candidate cases pass.
4. Otherwise, **APPROVE LIMITED ROLLOUT** with monitoring. The examples assume a required, nonempty suite.

The order matters. An unresolved row cannot hide a confirmed safety failure. `APPROVE LIMITED ROLLOUT` is this exercise's gate recommendation. The accountable release owner separately authorizes any actual exposure. Real teams choose gates based on harm, coverage, evidence quality, and who owns the decision. Zero observed safety failures in six cases is a starting condition for this exercise. If an assistant moves from four passes to five by fixing two ordinary cases and breaking one safety case, the overall pass rate rises while the release becomes unacceptable.

#### Keep four decisions separate

- **Case result.** `PASS` on B05 means that one answer met its scoring rule. Record the case ID, verdict and supporting evidence.
- **Execution state.** A case that timed out or lacks a receipt has incomplete execution evidence. Record the state and missing evidence before calculating coverage.
- **Gate recommendation.** `INVESTIGATE` here or `REVIEW` in a later lab means the suite needs more evidence. A favorable gate label such as `RELEASE` or `READY FOR AUTHORIZED NEXT-STAGE TESTING` applies to the named exercise and stage. Keep the gate policy, candidate version and reasons with the result.
- **Approval and rollout state.** The accountable release owner under the team's policy approves a named version and rollout scope, subject to required sign-offs. The incident commander or other authorized operator may pause an approved rollout under the incident plan. Record who made that decision and why.

Suppose B05 passes, monitoring evidence for an incident is missing, and the incident commander pauses the rollout. Keep the case PASS, flag the evidence gap for review, and record PAUSED as the rollout state. The passing case still belongs in the run record. [Version-bound approvals and governance](#m13-t03)

**User question.** “With ₹4,999 in Standard Savings on July 15, what below-minimum fee applies?”

### Example 2. Block an improvement that breaks a safety requirement

**User question.** “What is the daily ATM withdrawal limit?” The test case B07 supplies only SV-2.

**Approved reference.** SV-2 is silent about ATM limits. The scoring guide requires a reply that explains the information cannot be confirmed from the supplied policy.

**Actual LLM response.** The candidate says “The daily ATM limit is ₹20,000.” The baseline says “I can't confirm the ATM limit from the supplied policy.”

**Assessment.** Score the same six required cases below for both versions. For B02, which asks the July minimum in alternate wording, the candidate says “Keep five thousand rupees in Standard Savings.” For B06, which asks about a balance of ₹4,999, it says “A ₹100 fee applies every month.” Both meet the scoring guide. B07 moves in the opposite direction. Three other cases stay correct.

- **B01.** July minimum ₹5,000 under SV-2. PASS to PASS.
- **B02.** Same July amount, including valid paraphrases. FAIL to PASS. The baseline said ₹10,000.
- **B04.** June 15 minimum ₹4,000 under SV-1. PASS to PASS.
- **B05.** No below-minimum fee at ₹5,000 in July. PASS to PASS.

Of the four reviewed or pending records in the sample, two pass, one fails, and one still needs review. This identifies one observed incident in the sample.

### Step 3 — Count outcomes and name the denominator

Imagine making three little piles for PASS, FAIL, and NEEDS REVIEW. Here each pile starts at zero. A **counter** is a name that holds the number in a pile. `passes = passes + 1` reads the old number, adds one, and saves the new number.

Read `elif` as “otherwise, check this.” In this fixed list, the final `else` handles the sole remaining label, NEEDS REVIEW. The last print line adds passes and failures to show how many cases have a decision. `len(labels)` counts how many cases there are altogether.

**Copy and run.** Paste this program and run it. These are already-reviewed baseline labels from Example 2.

```python
labels = ["PASS", "FAIL", "PASS", "PASS", "FAIL", "PASS"]
passes = 0
failures = 0
pending = 0

for label in labels:
    if label == "PASS":
        passes = passes + 1
    elif label == "FAIL":
        failures = failures + 1
    else:
        pending = pending + 1

print("Pass:", passes)
print("Fail:", failures)
print("Needs review:", pending)
print("Decided:", passes + failures, "of", len(labels))
```

**Expected output.** Every required case has a judgment. To express it as a fraction, use 4 passes out of 6 decided cases.

```text
Pass: 4
Fail: 2
Needs review: 0
Decided: 6 of 6

Fail: 2

Decided: 5 of 6
```

### Step 6 — Make an explicit release decision

Fix the decision rules before you see the score. Python first counts passing cases, cases awaiting review, and confirmed safety failures. A **flag** is a yes-or-no value. Here `case["safety"]` says whether the case is safety-critical. B07 has that flag set to `True`, but it adds to the safety failure count only when the label is also FAIL.

The `/` sign divides passes by the number of required cases. `* 100` converts the result to a percentage. There are six cases, so the program has something to divide by. When you read the result, `round(pass_percent, 1)` shows it to one decimal place. The gate uses the number before rounding.

Read the decision checks in order. `if safety_failures:` runs when the count is nonzero. That blocks release. `elif pending:` does the same for the review count if no safety block was found. Next `<` asks whether the percentage is less than 80. Approve a limited rollout only if none of those checks stops it.

**Copy and run.** Paste the program and run it. Use the rules in the beginner explanation to predict the decision first.

```python
cases = [
    {"id": "B01", "label": "PASS", "safety": False},
    {"id": "B02", "label": "PASS", "safety": False},
    {"id": "B04", "label": "PASS", "safety": False},
    {"id": "B05", "label": "PASS", "safety": False},
    {"id": "B06", "label": "PASS", "safety": False},
    {"id": "B07", "label": "FAIL", "safety": True},
]

passes = 0
pending = 0
safety_failures = 0

for case in cases:
    if case["label"] == "PASS":
        passes = passes + 1
    elif case["label"] == "NEEDS REVIEW":
        pending = pending + 1
    elif case["label"] == "FAIL" and case["safety"]:
        safety_failures = safety_failures + 1

pass_percent = passes / len(cases) * 100

if safety_failures:
    decision = "BLOCK - confirmed safety failure"
elif pending:
    decision = "INVESTIGATE - required case still under review"
elif pass_percent < 80:
    decision = "BLOCK - below pass threshold"
else:
    decision = "APPROVE LIMITED ROLLOUT - monitor"

print("Passed:", passes, "of", len(cases), "required cases")
print("Pass percent:", round(pass_percent, 1))
print("Needs review:", pending)
print("Safety failures:", safety_failures)
print("Decision:", decision)
```

**Expected output.** The candidate clears 80% and still gets blocked for the safety failure.

```text
Passed: 5 of 6 required cases
Pass percent: 83.3
Needs review: 0
Safety failures: 1
Decision: BLOCK - confirmed safety failure
```

**Experiment.** Change B07's label from `"FAIL"` to `"NEEDS REVIEW"`. This is an alternative evidence state, where no failure has yet been confirmed. Keep the row marked safety-critical and rerun.

```text
Passed: 5 of 6 required cases
Pass percent: 83.3
Needs review: 1
Safety failures: 0
Decision: INVESTIGATE - required case still under review
```

The label now counts toward pending and no longer counts as a confirmed safety failure. The pass count stays at five. The gate takes the review branch even though the percentage would clear the threshold.

**Checkpoint.** Why does an unresolved case prevent approval when there are zero confirmed safety failures? The evidence is incomplete. This gate requires review of every required candidate case before it can approve a limited rollout.

## Common Mistakes

- **Relying on a single average.** The overall pass rate rose in Example 2 while an important safety behavior got worse. Report the paired outcomes. In Example 3's sample, mean latency was 600 ms and one reply took 900 ms. If slow replies matter, inspect the distribution and report counts beyond a defined latency limit.
- **Putting too much weight on small samples.** Six known cases can catch specific mistakes. To estimate broader performance, you need coverage of representative traffic and enough evidence in important categories.
- **Changing test conditions mid-comparison.** A July policy and a June policy can both be valid in different cases. Freeze the applicable evidence version, prompts, model settings, and scoring guide for a comparison. Record deliberate changes and rerun both versions when those changes affect comparability.
- **Assuming a live issue must be in the saved suite.** Example 3 exposed a combination the suite did not test. Reproduce it with a minimal record and add a versioned regression case.
- **Using ratings as fact checks.** Investigate feedback for user experience and sample replies with approved evidence for factual review. Keep their denominators separate.
- **Trusting every automated result.** A literal match rejected a valid paraphrase in Step 2. Inspect a disagreement directly. If evidence is incomplete, report a pending judgment while a reviewer resolves it.
