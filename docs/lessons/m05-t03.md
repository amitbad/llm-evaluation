## Beginner Explanation

### Check one thing at a time

Compare the approved line with “You have up to thirty calendar days after your parcel arrives to ask for your money back. Bring a valid receipt.” The letters differ, but you can still find the right deadline, starting event, and receipt requirement.

An **evaluator** checks one or more qualities of a reply. An exact-match evaluator asks whether two strings are identical. It is useful when only one output is allowed, such as a specified refund status code. Here it reports different text for a paraphrase the store can use.

Now replace the approved line’s “30” with “90.” Eleven of its twelve distinct words still match. That fraction is a simple example of **lexical similarity**, which measures overlap in wording. A **semantic similarity** score estimates closeness in meaning. It can give a paraphrase a strong score and also give a wrong deadline a strong score because most of the sentence is still about the same rule. Similarity serves as a **proxy**, an indirect signal for what you really want to know. A score of 96 on a 0–100 scale is a measurement under that scoring method, with no established probability of correctness.

A direct **rule-based evaluator** can compare a reliably recorded day count with 30. A human reviewer can decide whether “first month” supplies a precise deadline, or whether “proof of purchase” promises to accept something the policy does not allow. Reviewers use the question, the reply, the authoritative policy, and a scoring guide. Two reviewers can still disagree. Keep their evidence and resolve the disputed criterion before treating a label as settled.

### Keep the individual results

Imagine reading “Request a refund within 30 calendar days of delivery.” The deadline check passes. The reply supplies no evidence about the required receipt. A high similarity score tells you it resembles the reference. The combination tells you where to look next.

Keep a **score vector**, the collection of results for a reply, before making a decision. It can contain both numbers and labels. For example, record exact match as DIFFERENT, word coverage as 9/12, semantic similarity as 96, deadline as PASS, and receipt as UNKNOWN. Each field measures a **dimension**, one aspect of the reply. Make it possible to see where the methods agreed, where they disagreed, and why.

Put the deadline result beside two questions. For “How long do I have?”, a reply that skips the deadline leaves you with UNKNOWN. For “Where is the refund help button?”, you never needed the deadline, so write N/A. A check that has a job in this case is **applicable**. If a scorer tries to check the first reply and times out, write ERROR for that scorer. Each label sends you somewhere different. Read the first reply again, check the button location for the second, or investigate the scorer that timed out.

### Use a score for the right job

Suppose a reply scores 96 for clarity and 80 for giving a useful next step. Harbor Leaf gives clarity three quarters of the influence and the next step one quarter. Three quarters of 96 is 72. One quarter of 80 is 20. Add them to get 92. This is a **weighted score**. The weights add to one, so scores on a 0–100 scale produce a summary on that same scale. Clarity here rates how easy the reply is to read. Usefulness rates whether it gives an actionable next step. The lesson supplies these ratings as fictional inputs.

Keep the summary focused on those two qualities. Counting exact match, word overlap, and semantic similarity as three votes for correctness would give repeated influence to related signals. Their agreement can still be useful as a debugging clue.

Suppose a reply gives 90 days, looks clear, and tells the customer to use the Returns button. It can still score 92 for presentation. The wrong deadline should stop it. A **hard gate** blocks acceptance when a critical check fails, regardless of the presentation score. Harbor Leaf uses hard gates for confirmed errors in the deadline or receipt statement. A gate needs trustworthy input. Ask for evidence when you cannot establish the fact it checks.

### Decide in a predictable order

Harbor Leaf uses the following decision table. An earlier row takes priority over a later row. A sequence where earlier rules can settle a case before later checks is a **cascade**. In this lesson the hard policy checks come first, followed by similarity triage. **Triage** means deciding which cases need a person’s attention.

| Evidence in the saved case | Decision | What happens next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An applicable policy fact is confirmed wrong | REJECT | Record the mismatch and hold the reply. |
| A required fact is unresolved or its check is unavailable | REVIEW | Retrieve the approved source, inspect the wording, or repair the check. |
| Semantic similarity is required but the score is unavailable or below the preset cutoff | REVIEW | Inspect the saved reply or rerun the scorer. |
| Presentation is below 80 | REVIEW | Ask a reviewer to inspect clarity and the next step. |
| All applicable checks clear their rules | ACCEPT | Record which checks supported acceptance. |

Routing version `routing-v1` uses 85 as its semantic cutoff, and values at least 85 clear that check. The value is an illustrative choice. A slightly lower score should prioritize review under this policy. It cannot prove a factual error. A question with an unclear purchase channel, a reference lacking its effective date, or a phrase such as “first month” needs better evidence. If the evidence already proves a violation, an unavailable similarity score does not erase it.

Suppose seven cases arrive and the system makes three automatic decisions. The automatic decision **coverage** is 3 out of 7. If all three agree with independent human outcomes, the observed automatic error count is 0 out of 3 checked decisions. Divide by every case for coverage and only by checked automatic decisions for the error rate. The other four require attention. At an assumed four minutes each, the review queue takes 16 minutes. Lowering a cutoff may reduce that workload and can also admit additional mistakes. Compare both quantities on fixed replies and review the cases that moved.

### Leave the next reviewer enough evidence

Save the case ID, question, reference and source version, captured reply, individual evaluator outputs, routing version, automatic decision, reason, and any later reviewer outcome. This is an **audit record** because another person can trace the decision using the same inputs. Keep the initial route even after a reviewer resolves the case. Changing the routing version can change the automatic decision without changing what the assistant said.

## Worked Demonstration

### Example 1. The checks disagree about two refund replies

**User question.** For an eligible item, how long do I have to request a refund and do I need my receipt?

**Approved reference.** Under `refund-1`, “Request a refund within 30 calendar days of delivery with a valid receipt.”

**Actual LLM response.** A says “You have up to thirty calendar days after your parcel arrives to ask for your money back. Bring a valid receipt.” B repeats the approved wording with “90” in place of “30.” Both replies are fictional.

**Assessment.** Exact match reports DIFFERENT for both. Counting unique reference words after removing case and punctuation gives A five matches out of twelve and B eleven out of twelve. The supplied semantic scores are 92 and 98. Then check the policy facts. A preserves the delivery event, 30-day deadline, and valid receipt requirement. B makes an explicit wrong deadline claim, even though its receipt statement is right.

**Failure classification.** With presentation above 80 for both, the cascade can accept A and rejects B. Exact match and word overlap explain how the surface changed. The direct comparison with the approved text supports the release decision. These similarity scores describe the fictional case only.

### Example 2. A presentation score cannot clear a failed gate

**User question.** How do I start a refund, and what is the deadline for this eligible item?

**Approved reference.** The deadline is 30 calendar days from delivery with a valid receipt. Use the same deadline and eligibility rule as in Example 1.

**Actual LLM response.** B says “Request a refund within 90 calendar days of delivery with a valid receipt.”

**Assessment.** Give the reply the supplied clarity rating of 96 and next-step usefulness rating of 80. The presentation score is 0.75 × 96 + 0.25 × 80 = 92. Imagine instead averaging its semantic score of 98, presentation of 92, and a deadline failure coded as 0. The average is 190 ÷ 3 ≈ 63.3. An illustrative cutoff of 60 would accept it. The actual decision table compares the recorded 90 with the approved 30 first and rejects it.

**Failure classification.** Hold the draft and tell the writer exactly what needs fixing. The 92 still says the draft was easy to read and gave a useful next step. Ask the writer to keep those strengths and change the deadline from 90 to 30. Simply moving the average cutoff would leave the wrong claim in the reply.

### Example 3. High scores leave an unresolved deadline

**User question.** What is the deadline for an eligible order, and what do I need?

**Approved reference.** The saved reference quotes 30 calendar days after delivery and a valid receipt, under `refund-1`.

**Actual LLM response.** C says “During the first month after arrival, ask for your money back with a valid receipt.”

**Assessment.** The sample semantic score is 94. Its presentation score is 0.75 × 92 + 0.25 × 88 = 91. The receipt is verified. “First month” supplies no unambiguous calendar-day count, so the deadline stays UNKNOWN. Route C to REVIEW even though the scores clear their cutoffs. Ask a reviewer to check the effective policy for this order and determine whether the local help text gives “first month” a precise definition. Preserve the original reply while doing so.

**Failure classification.** The audit row reads `C | refund-1 | routing-v1 | deadline UNKNOWN | receipt PASS | semantic 94 | presentation 91 | REVIEW: deadline | human outcome PENDING`. If the appropriate source cannot resolve the phrase, the reviewer can record that the reply needs rewriting before release. The available evidence does not establish that the assistant explicitly claimed a wrong number.

### Step 1 — Compare exact match with shared words

See how two checks react to the same paraphrase. `==` compares both full strings. `lower()` makes the letters lowercase, `replace(".", "")` removes periods, and `split()` separates words at spaces. `set()` keeps one copy of each word. `&` finds the words in both sets.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
reference = "Request a refund within 30 calendar days of delivery with a valid receipt."
candidate = "You have up to thirty calendar days after your parcel arrives to ask for your money back. Bring a valid receipt."

def words(text):
    return set(text.lower().replace(".", "").split())

expected = words(reference)
shared = expected & words(candidate)
print(f"Exact match: {candidate == reference}")
print(f"Distinct reference words shared: {len(shared)}/{len(expected)}")
```

```text
Distinct reference words shared: 5/12
```

### Step 7 — Build an auditable cascade

Combine the earlier checks on seven saved replies. The program computes exact match, word coverage, policy verdicts, presentation, and routing. The semantic numbers and two presentation ratings on each row are fictional inputs. The last value is a separate, fictional human disposition. It lets you check the automatic decisions. It never enters the automatic routing function.

A case has a task so the program knows which question and reference to use. A receipt value of `True` means it was verified that the reply requires a valid receipt. `False` means the reply explicitly waives it. `None` means the required fact is unresolved. For the navigation task, the day and receipt checks are N/A. The approved help location still matters, so its check uses the fixed reference route. In this exercise the navigation reply has exactly that wording. A reference to the correct route in other words would go to review. The task, rather than the score label, tells the router whether semantic similarity is required.

The policy checks use only verified calendar-day counts from delivery. A dictionary stores values under names such as `"question"`. `record["decision"]` looks up the decision. In `90 * 0.75 + 80 * 0.25`, the shares total one. The `for` loop saves each record and prints its route. The square brackets in `auto` collect records whose decision is ACCEPT or REJECT. `checked` keeps just the automatic decisions with a human outcome. `sum()` counts disagreements by adding one for each true comparison.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
reference = "Request a refund within 30 calendar days of delivery with a valid receipt."
help_reference = "Open Orders and select Contact support for the purchase."
refund_policy = "refund-1"
routing_version = "routing-v1"
cutoff = {"routing-v1": 85, "routing-v2": 80, "routing-v3": 95}[routing_version]
questions = {
    "refund": "What is the refund deadline and what do I need?",
    "help": "Where is the refund help button?",
}
cases = [
    ("A", "refund", "You have up to thirty calendar days after your parcel arrives to ask for your money back. Bring a valid receipt.", 30, True, 92, 96, 80, "ACCEPT"),
    ("B", "refund", "Request a refund within 90 calendar days of delivery with a valid receipt.", 90, True, 98, 96, 80, "REJECT"),
    ("C", "refund", "During the first month after arrival, ask for your money back with a valid receipt.", None, True, 94, 92, 88, "PENDING"),
    ("D", "refund", "Request a refund within 30 calendar days of delivery.", 30, None, 96, 96, 88, "REJECT"),
    ("E", "help", help_reference, None, None, "N/A", 80, 92, "ACCEPT"),
    ("F", "refund", reference, 30, True, "ERROR", 88, 88, "ACCEPT"),
    ("G", "refund", "Keep the valid receipt. Count 30 calendar days from when the package arrives to request your refund.", 30, True, 83, 88, 92, "ACCEPT"),
]

def words(text):
    return set(text.lower().replace(".", "").replace(",", "").split())

def route(deadline, receipt, help_route, semantic_required, semantic, presentation):
    if "FAIL" in (deadline, receipt):
        return "REJECT", "policy mismatch"
    if "UNKNOWN" in (deadline, receipt, help_route):
        return "REVIEW", "policy evidence"
    if semantic_required and semantic == "ERROR":
        return "REVIEW", "scorer error"
    if semantic_required and semantic == "N/A":
        return "REVIEW", "missing semantic score"
    if semantic_required and semantic < cutoff:
        return "REVIEW", "low semantic score"
    if presentation < 80:
        return "REVIEW", "presentation"
    return "ACCEPT", "checks cleared"

records = []
for name, task, reply, days, valid_receipt, semantic, clarity, usefulness, human in cases:
    approved = reference if task == "refund" else help_reference
    exact = reply == approved
    shared = len(words(reply) & words(approved))
    deadline = receipt = help_route = "N/A"
    if task == "refund":
        deadline = "UNKNOWN" if days is None else ("PASS" if days == 30 else "FAIL")
        receipt = "UNKNOWN" if valid_receipt is None else ("PASS" if valid_receipt else "FAIL")
    else:
        help_route = "PASS" if exact else "UNKNOWN"

    presentation = 0.75 * clarity + 0.25 * usefulness
    decision, reason = route(deadline, receipt, help_route, task == "refund", semantic, presentation)
    record = {
        "id": name, "question": questions[task], "reference": approved, "candidate": reply,
        "refund_policy": refund_policy, "routing_version": routing_version,
        "exact": exact, "shared": shared, "reference_words": len(words(approved)),
        "deadline": deadline, "receipt": receipt, "help_route": help_route, "semantic": semantic,
        "clarity": clarity, "usefulness": usefulness, "presentation": presentation,
        "decision": decision, "reason": reason, "human": human,
    }
    records.append(record)
    print(f"{name}: {decision} ({reason}) | semantic {semantic} | presentation {presentation:.2f} | human {human}")

auto = [r for r in records if r["decision"] != "REVIEW"]
checked = [r for r in auto if r["human"] != "PENDING"]
errors = sum(r["decision"] != r["human"] for r in checked)
print(f"{routing_version} | semantic cutoff {cutoff}")
print(f"Auto coverage: {len(auto)}/{len(records)} | errors: {errors}/{len(checked)}")
print(f"Review queue: {len(records) - len(auto)}")
audit = records[2]
print(f'Audit {audit["id"]} | {audit["refund_policy"]} | {audit["routing_version"]} | deadline {audit["deadline"]} | receipt {audit["receipt"]}')
print(f'Reference: {audit["reference"]}')
print(f'Candidate: {audit["candidate"]}')
```

```text
B: REJECT (policy mismatch) | semantic 98 | presentation 92.00 | human REJECT
C: REVIEW (policy evidence) | semantic 94 | presentation 91.00 | human PENDING
D: REVIEW (policy evidence) | semantic 96 | presentation 94.00 | human REJECT
E: ACCEPT (checks cleared) | semantic N/A | presentation 83.00 | human ACCEPT
```

## Knowledge Check

1. Exact match rejects a correct paraphrase. When would exact match be an appropriate authority?
2. Three wording-oriented scores all rise after a rewrite. Why might giving them each a separate vote overstate the evidence?
3. You increase the weight of the higher presentation rating. What happens to the score if the ratings remain fixed?
4. A receipt statement is confirmed false and similarity is unavailable. Which branch should run first?
5. Why should the deadline check for a help-button question use N/A?
6. A case with a strong aggregate says “first month.” What evidence would justify taking it out of review?
7. How would you report a replay with three automatic decisions, one of them awaiting a human label, and no errors among the other two?
8. Exactly the same reply moves to acceptance when the routing version changes. What must the team preserve to explain the move?

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. Use it when the agreed task requires one exact output, such as a fixed status code. A policy answer in prose can express the same facts with different words.
2. These measures all react to properties of the same wording. Their agreement can be informative, but it gives no independent evidence about whether the answer matches the policy.
3. The aggregate rises, because a larger share goes to the higher rating. The change describes the scoring rule.
4. Reject at the policy gate. The explicit contradiction is already supported by the approved source. Log the unavailable similarity separately.
5. The question concerns a user-interface location. A deadline would answer a different question. N/A says the check had no job in this case, so it should neither hurt the score nor disappear as a timeout.
6. Check the authoritative policy version and the context for that order. If that evidence gives an unambiguous 30-calendar-day meaning and supports the other facts, document it. If it leaves the deadline unclear, keep or resolve the review by requiring a rewrite.
7. Report zero errors out of two checked automatic decisions and one automatic decision awaiting a label. Give coverage as three out of the total number of attempted cases if that total is available.
8. Keep the captured reply, question, reference, policy version, original scores, earlier decision, and both routing versions. The difference can then be traced to the actual rule change.

## Common Mistakes

Mixing hard requirements into one average can hide bad advice. Check the policy facts first, then use presentation and similarity for their stated jobs.

A missing input, a scorer error, and a check that does not apply deserve separate labels. Otherwise you cannot tell whether to inspect the reply, fix the evaluator, or leave the check out of that task.

A smaller review queue can come with more mistakes. When a rule changes, compare automatic errors and coverage on the same cases and include the number of checked labels. Write down any assumed review cost so another person can recalculate it.
