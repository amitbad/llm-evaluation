Then someone opens the policy and finds a 30-day deadline. The customer is already too late. Before trusting the next round of ratings, the team needs to understand how three matching judgments missed the same fact.

Each customer in the fixture has an eligible unused online order and asks for the deadline and what they need to request a refund. The approved clause is the reference for those answers.

## Beginner Explanation

### Start with two independent reads

Nia and Omar each receive the same policy, question, and saved replies. They record their labels without seeing each other’s work or the judge’s output. This is **independent review**. A **rubric** is their shared scoring guide. Version `v1` says PASS for “sufficient refund guidance,” FAIL for wrong or insufficient guidance, and REVIEW for a material doubt. It does not say clearly whether omitting the receipt makes a reply insufficient. A rubric anchor is a concrete example showing which label to assign and why.

| ID | Frozen reply | Nia | Omar | Judge replay v1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | You may request a refund within 30 calendar days after delivery with a valid receipt. | PASS | PASS | PASS |
| B | You can request a refund within 90 calendar days after delivery with a valid receipt. | PASS | PASS | PASS |
| C | Ask for a refund during the first month after delivery if you have proof of purchase. | REVIEW | PASS | PASS |
| D | You can request a refund within 30 calendar days after delivery. | FAIL | PASS | PASS |
| E | Contact our support team for assistance. | FAIL | FAIL | FAIL |

Put a check mark next to each row where Nia and Omar gave the same label. You’ll mark A and B for matching PASS labels, and E for matching FAIL labels. C and D get no mark. You have three marks across five rows that both people rated. Divide 3 by 5 to get 60%. That is **exact agreement**. It counts matches, so on a five-point scale a 4 versus a 5 is a mismatch just as a 1 versus a 5 is.

The judge matches Nia on three cases and Omar on all five. Nia and Omar use different interpretations of the receipt rule. Matching one person more often does not tell you whose interpretation is supported. Check each disagreement using the frozen reply and the source. Then inspect some agreements too. B is where all three labels miss the same fact.

### Confirm what the policy supports

Put B’s “90 calendar days” beside the approved “through calendar day 30.” At day 45, the two statements give different instructions. The written policy provides stronger evidence than the three matching labels. An independent reviewer can now record FAIL for this specific claim.

A person who resolves a disputed judgment by reading the evidence and applying an agreed rule is doing **adjudication**. Keep their reason alongside their decision. For D, the updated rubric can make the omission rule explicit and support FAIL. For C, there is no exact day count and no clear confirmation of a valid receipt. Leave its adjudicated label open and route it for clarification. REVIEW describes the next action. A pending reference means the team has yet to establish a binary outcome for this report.

Report judge agreement with each reviewer separately. Then compare valid judge labels against adjudicated labels when available. The second comparison estimates correctness against a documented reference on those cases. A reference label is as trustworthy as the evidence and rule used to establish it. A judge output that cannot be read under the required format is invalid. In Step 7 that format is one label, so the illustrative raw value “{broken” cannot be scored. Keep it out of agreement and report it separately.

### Read the types of mistakes

Imagine testing a held-out group with two adjudicated PASS cases and two adjudicated FAIL cases. The saved judge gets one of each right. Write the reference labels down the side and judge labels across the top. This count table is a **confusion matrix**.

| Adjudicated label | Judge PASS | Judge FAIL |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PASS | 1 | 1 |
| FAIL | 1 | 1 |

The PASS/PASS and FAIL/FAIL cells give 2 matches out of 4. The FAIL row has one false pass out of two known FAIL replies. The PASS row has one false fail out of two known PASS replies. A judge that passes every reply can look strong on a set dominated by correct replies. Read each row before trusting the total. Report the number of cases, the number with both valid binary labels, and the cases excluded because of missing or invalid evidence.

### Notice how the choice of labels affects agreement

Picture eight new refund replies. Nia and Omar both use PASS six times and FAIL twice. They match on six replies, giving **observed agreement** of 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75. If you shuffled each reviewer’s labels independently while keeping their label counts, you would still expect many PASS labels to land together. The expected agreement under this independence calculation is the product of the two PASS frequencies plus the product of the two FAIL frequencies. Here it is (6/8 × 6/8) + (2/8 × 2/8) = 0.625.

**Cohen’s kappa** compares the observed agreement to that baseline. The observed result is 0.75 − 0.625 = 0.125 above it. The best possible agreement is 1, so the available room was 1 − 0.625 = 0.375. Divide 0.125 by 0.375 to get 0.333. In symbols, κ = (observed − expected) ÷ (1 − expected). A kappa of 0 means the match rate equals this baseline. A negative value means fewer matches than the baseline expects. If expected agreement is 1, kappa is undefined. [Cohen, A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001316446002000104)

If both reviewers used PASS and FAIL equally often with the same 75% observed agreement, expected agreement would be 0.50, and kappa would be 0.50. The frequency of a category in the set is its **prevalence**. Show the label counts, sample size, raw agreement, and kappa together. Kappa describes agreement under a particular baseline. It cannot tell you whether either person applied the refund policy correctly.

### Calibrate the rule, then test it on fresh cases

Choose practice cases that cover clear answers, wrong deadlines, missing receipts, and ambiguous replies. Have the reviewers label them independently. Compare their ratings and read the disputed text against the policy. Write down which part of the guide caused the disagreement. For instance, replace “sufficient refund guidance” with examples of a complete answer, a missing receipt, and an uncertain date. Save the resolved labels and any pending cases alongside a new rubric version. This is **calibration** in this lesson.

Keep a separate set for evaluation. A **holdout** is a set you keep away from the examples used to revise the rubric or judge prompt. Keep near-duplicate replies from the same question family in the same partition. Reviewers can prepare reference labels independently, while the people revising the rubric avoid reading the holdout cases or scores until the rules are frozen. Opening the same outcomes after each edit lets you tailor the rule to those particular replies. Using held-out examples to pick a rule moves information into the design process. That is **data leakage**. If you revise the rules after reading the results, treat those cases as development evidence and use a fresh holdout.

### Test a suspected preference without changing the facts

Take a correct short reply and a longer version with the same facts. Give both the same question, policy, rubric, and judge settings. If the longer wording repeatedly gets a better verdict, investigate a possible preference for verbosity. Try adding “Definitely” to a wrong reply to test confidence. Show the same two policy facts once in a paragraph and once as a numbered list to test formatting. Compare a plain reply with a polished paraphrase that keeps the deadline and receipt unchanged. Change one property at a time. Keep the review focused on the refund requirements unless the rubric explicitly includes style. A **slice** is a subgroup, such as short or long replies, whose errors you report separately.

An evaluator may also favor replies generated by its own model family. This is **model self-preference**. For example, the judge may pass its own model family’s replies more often than equally accurate replies written by another family. Keep model identities hidden when possible and compare independently labeled examples from several sources. Check whether the pattern persists after matching on facts and controlling presentation. If you present multiple answers together, their order may affect judgments. Here each replay rates one reply independently. For pointwise **repeat consistency**, rerun that same reply with the same inputs and count how often the verdict changes. Repeat agreement can coexist with a repeated mistake. [Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/91f18a1287b398d378ef22505bf41832-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.html)

A report should keep each case ID, frozen reply, policy, dataset version, split, reviewer IDs and labels, raw judge output, rubric version, disagreement reason, adjudicated outcome, and calibration action. Include the counts and denominators behind each summary. Save each new run alongside the earlier one. This report computes agreement from the saved per-case labels so a colleague can reproduce the calculation.

### Example 1. Count the matches before asking why

**User question.** How long after delivery can I ask for a refund, and what do I need?

**Approved reference.** Under `refund-1` the window ends on calendar day 30 and a valid receipt is required.

**Actual LLM response.** D says “You can request a refund within 30 calendar days after delivery.” Read D with A, B, C, and E in the calibration table.

**Assessment.** Nia and Omar each labeled all five replies separately. A, B, and E match, giving 3/5 or 60% agreement. C and D differ. On D, Omar treated the deadline as sufficient guidance, while Nia considered the receipt a required part of the answer.

**Failure classification.** Keep both original labels. Record an omission under the clarified rubric and add an anchor that names the missing receipt as a failure for this question. The 60% describes this table and cannot explain the disagreement by itself.

### Example 2. Find an error inside unanimous agreement

**User question.** How long after delivery can I request a refund, and what do I need?

**Approved reference.** The approved source allows a request through calendar day 30 after delivery with a valid receipt.

**Actual LLM response.** B says “You can request a refund within 90 calendar days after delivery with a valid receipt.”

**Assessment.** Nia, Omar, and the judge replay all use PASS. Read the cutoff directly from the reply and the source. At day 45, B says the request is within the window, but the approved clause says the window has ended. Record an adjudicated FAIL under the same ID.

**Failure classification.** Write two lines for B. On the agreement line, all three pairs of raters matched: Nia and Omar, Nia and the judge, Omar and the judge. On the policy line, all three PASS labels conflict with the 30-day rule. Keep the original labels, block the reply, and record the evidence for FAIL. Use B in calibration to show why a similar format and a correct receipt condition cannot rescue a wrong deadline.

### Example 3. Investigate which answers earned undeserved credit

**User question.** For the same eligible order, how long do I have and what do I need to request a refund?

**Approved reference.** Use `refund-1`. A PASS answers both required facts. A wrong deadline is FAIL even when the answer is polished.

**Actual LLM response.** The frozen holdout has F and G, short and long versions of a correct answer for a blender order, and H and I, short and long versions of the same wrong 90-day answer for a jacket order. J is ambiguous. K has an invalid replay output. The exact texts and outputs are in Step 7.

**Assessment.** The first replay labels F and H FAIL and G and I PASS. Among comparable cases it scores 1/2 on short replies and 1/2 on long replies. The short error is a false fail and the long error is a false pass. Revise the prompt on the separate calibration set, then check saved holdout outputs for the revised guide. The fictional replay changes F to PASS and I to FAIL and leaves G and H correct. Comparable cases rise from two correct out of four to four out of four. J and K stay outside that comparison.

**Failure classification.** The first replay is consistent with a possible length preference on these pairs. The second reduces the observed errors in this teaching set. Keep the two runs, fixtures, and evidence notes linked to their versions. Test the pattern on more matched examples before claiming a general preference or better performance from a live judge.

### Step 2 — Calculate exact agreement

Count matches out of all comparable pairs in the first table. In this activity REVIEW counts as a recorded label, and every case has both ratings. When Python adds comparisons with `sum()`, each `True` counts as one. `len()` counts pairs. In an `f"..."` string, `:.1f` displays one decimal place.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
pairs = [("PASS", "PASS"), ("PASS", "PASS"), ("REVIEW", "PASS"),
         ("FAIL", "PASS"), ("FAIL", "FAIL")]
matches = sum(nia == omar for nia, omar in pairs)
total = len(pairs)

print(f"Matches: {matches}/{total}")
print(f"Exact agreement: {100 * matches / total:.1f}%")
```

```text
Matches: 3/5
Exact agreement: 60.0%
```

**Experiment.** Change Omar’s `PASS` to `FAIL` only in the pair `("FAIL", "PASS")`.

```text
Matches: 4/5
Exact agreement: 80.0%
```

One additional pair matches and the denominator stays five. **Checkpoint.** Does removing a disputed row give the same result? Removing D instead would give 3/4, or 75%, on a different set. Document exclusions rather than using them to raise the headline percentage.

### Step 5 — Adjust agreement for label frequency

Use the eight fresh examples from the kappa calculation. The four counts say how often each pair of labels occurred. The sum gives the number of pairs. Divide the row and column totals by that number to find each reviewer’s label frequencies. Multiply matching frequencies to get expected agreement, then subtract it from observed agreement before dividing by the remaining room.

[Open Python Editor](https://www.online-python.com/)

```python
both_pass = 5
nia_pass_omar_fail = 1
nia_fail_omar_pass = 1
both_fail = 1

total = both_pass + nia_pass_omar_fail + nia_fail_omar_pass + both_fail
observed = (both_pass + both_fail) / total
nia_pass = (both_pass + nia_pass_omar_fail) / total
omar_pass = (both_pass + nia_fail_omar_pass) / total
expected = nia_pass * omar_pass + (1 - nia_pass) * (1 - omar_pass)

print(f"Observed agreement: {observed:.3f}")
print(f"Expected agreement: {expected:.4f}")
if expected == 1:
    print("Kappa: undefined")
else:
    print(f"Kappa: {(observed - expected) / (1 - expected):.3f}")
```

```text
Observed agreement: 0.750
Expected agreement: 0.6250
Kappa: 0.333
```

**Experiment.** Reassign one pair from Nia FAIL/Omar PASS to both FAIL, leaving eight cases. Replace the four consecutive count assignments with `both_pass, nia_pass_omar_fail, nia_fail_omar_pass, both_fail = 5, 1, 0, 2`.

```text
Observed agreement: 0.875
Expected agreement: 0.5625
Kappa: 0.714
```

The reassignment keeps Nia at six PASS labels and moves Omar from six to five. The two expected contributions become 30/64 and 6/64. Observed agreement is 7/8, so kappa is (0.875 − 0.5625) ÷ 0.4375 ≈ 0.714. **Checkpoint.** If both reviewers use only PASS, what does the program print? Expected agreement becomes 1, so kappa is undefined even with unanimous PASS. Record the raw counts and inspect the sample mix.

### Step 7 — Replay the calibration and audit report

Use frozen cases and two dictionaries of fictional replay labels. Each key is a case ID. A through E were available during calibration. F through K are the holdout. Both replay versions use identical candidate texts and independent human ratings. The fictional `v2` guide explicitly requires the deadline and receipt, fails contradictions and material omissions, sends ambiguity to REVIEW, and asks the judge to ignore tone and length. The `v2` labels are supplied for comparison. The Python code does not generate them.

Each case row contains the reply, style, two original human labels, a reviewed day count when available, and any adjudicated binary label. F/G and H/I share a ticket within each pair. The ticket check keeps both versions of each answer in the same split. A reason is stored even when adjudication remains pending. `set(calibration_ids).isdisjoint(holdout_ids)` checks that no ID belongs to both groups. A set comparison checks that all cases are assigned. In the report, the tuple key `(reference, judge)` keeps the two axes of the confusion matrix in that order.

For judge-human agreement, a valid REVIEW counts as a label when comparing raw ratings. For the binary confusion matrix, a case needs both an adjudicated PASS or FAIL and a valid judge PASS or FAIL. Missing sources, unresolved references, valid judge abstentions, and malformed verdicts need their own counts or reasons. The policy check uses day counts already checked for unit and starting event. The omitted receipt on D is recorded in the adjudicator’s note.

        "adjudicated": reference, "reference_note": note,
        "human_dispute": nia != omar,
        "calibration_action": "add anchors for deadline, receipt, and ambiguity",
    }

print(f"{dataset} | rubric {rubric}")
for group, ids in [("Calibration", calibration_ids), ("Holdout", holdout_ids)]:
    rows = [audit[case_id] for case_id in ids]
    valid = [r for r in rows if r["judge"] is not None]
    comparable = [r for r in valid if r["adjudicated"] in binary and r["judge"] in binary]
    human_matches = sum(r["Nia"] == r["Omar"] for r in rows)
    judge_nia = sum(r["judge"] == r["Nia"] for r in valid)
    judge_omar = sum(r["judge"] == r["Omar"] for r in valid)
    matches = sum(r["judge"] == r["adjudicated"] for r in comparable)
    unresolved = [r["id"] for r in rows if r["adjudicated"] is None]
    invalid = [r["id"] for r in rows if r["judge"] is None]
    disputes = [r["id"] for r in rows if r["human_dispute"]]
    print(f"{group} n={len(rows)}: human {human_matches}/{len(rows)} | judge vs Nia {judge_nia}/{len(valid)} | judge vs Omar {judge_omar}/{len(valid)}")
    print(f"  valid {len(valid)}/{len(rows)} | adjudicated matches {matches}/{len(comparable)}")
    print(f"  disputed {','.join(disputes)} | unresolved {','.join(unresolved)} | invalid {','.join(invalid) or 'none'}")
    if group == "Holdout":
        matrix = {(ref, pred): 0 for ref in binary for pred in binary}
        for r in comparable:
            matrix[r["adjudicated"], r["judge"]] += 1
        for ref in binary:
            print(f"  ref {ref}: judge PASS {matrix[ref, 'PASS']} | judge FAIL {matrix[ref, 'FAIL']}")
        for ref, pred, error in [("FAIL", "PASS", "False passes"), ("PASS", "FAIL", "False fails")]:
            total = sum(matrix[ref, label] for label in binary)
            count = matrix[ref, pred]
            rate = f"{count}/{total} ({100 * count / total:.1f}%)" if total else "unavailable (0 comparable)"
            print(f"  {error}: {rate}")
        for style in ("short", "long"):
            members = [r for r in comparable if r["style"] == style]
            correct = sum(r["judge"] == r["adjudicated"] for r in members)
            print(f"  {style}: {correct}/{len(members)} adjudicated matches")

for case_id in ("B", "I"):
    r = audit[case_id]
    print(f"Audit {case_id}: refund-1 | deadline {r['deadline_check']} | judge {r['judge']} | adjudicated {r['adjudicated']} | {r['reference_note']}")

## Knowledge Check Answers

1. Omar may have been influenced by the visible marks. Preserve independent rating conditions so their match rate reflects separately reached decisions.
2. It is 6/8, or 75%. Exact agreement counts every unequal pair once and does not encode the size of an ordinal difference.
3. The unanimous rating tells you the reviewers and judge gave the same label. The documented rule supports a FAIL for the reply. Record the adjudication and investigate the shared miss.
4. One reviewer may have treated the old ambiguous language as permission to overlook a missing receipt. A clearer rule can move the judge away from that reviewer and closer to evidence-backed decisions.
5. Raw agreement is 100%. Expected agreement is 1, so kappa is undefined. Check whether the case mix includes instructive FAIL examples and independently verify the approved criteria.
6. The extra receipt changes the factual content. To investigate length, keep policy facts fixed within each pair before rewriting style.
7. Call the exposed cases development evidence and keep a new set sealed for evaluation. Retain the earlier result and connect each later run to its own prompt and data version.
8. Count a failed attempt in coverage, but omit the invalid output from substantive agreement and the matrix. Store the raw output, case ID, verdict failure reason, and retry or review action.

## Common Mistakes

Read some cases where everyone agreed. Shared assumptions or an overlooked fact can make all three labels wrong together.

Keep REVIEW, an unresolved reference, and an invalid judge output visible. Each has a different implication for the next action and the denominator.

Record which texts, reviewers, policy, and rubric produced each result. Keep calibration and holdout groups separate, and preserve earlier judgments when a new rubric changes the decision.
